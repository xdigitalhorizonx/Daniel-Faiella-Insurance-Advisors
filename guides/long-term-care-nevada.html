Long-Term Care · Nevada · 2026

Who pays when you need long-term care in Nevada?

Medicare's safety net for long-term care runs out fast. Here's what care actually costs in Northern Nevada, and the four real ways families cover it.

Talk through your options →

Most people plan for retirement around two questions: when can I stop working, and how will I cover health care? Long-term care is the third question almost nobody plans for — until a parent has a fall, or a fixed income can't stretch to cover a home health aide. Roughly 7 in 10 Americans who turn 65 will need some form of long-term care in their lifetime, and it is one of the few major expenses in retirement that Medicare mostly doesn't touch.

What long-term care actually costs in Nevada

"Long-term care" covers everything from a few hours of help at home to full-time care in a nursing home. In Northern Nevada, the going rates look roughly like this:

In-home care (part-time)~$20-$33/hour, billed as neededA caregiver for bathing, meals, mobility, a few hours a day
Assisted living~$5,000-$6,100/monthA private or shared apartment with daily living support on-site
Nursing home, semi-private room~$11,000/month24/7 skilled nursing care and supervision
Nursing home, private room~$12,800/monthSame as above, private room

Put another way: a semi-private nursing home room in Nevada runs well over $130,000 a year. A few years of care can undo decades of careful saving — which is exactly why this deserves a plan before it's needed, not during a crisis.

Where Medicare stops helping

This is the part that surprises the most people in my office. Medicare Part A will cover a skilled nursing facility stay — but only after a qualifying 3-day hospital admission, and only for rehabilitation, not ongoing custodial care. In 2026, that benefit breaks down as: days 1-20 at no cost, days 21-100 with a $217/day coinsurance, and nothing at all after day 100. There is no Medicare benefit for help with bathing, dressing, meals, or supervision at home or in assisted living — the kind of care most Nevadans actually end up needing, and needing for years, not weeks. A Medicare Supplement plan doesn't change this either; it only extends coverage on services Medicare already covers. This is a genuinely separate gap that has to be planned for on its own.

The four ways Nevada families actually pay for it

1. Pay out of pocket. Many families start here by necessity, drawing down savings, home equity, or investment income. It works, but it's the most expensive path and the one most likely to force hard decisions about a spouse's future security.

2. Traditional long-term care insurance. A standalone policy that pays a daily or monthly benefit toward covered care. Premiums are based on your age and health at the time you apply — buying in your mid-50s to early 60s, while you still qualify easily, is dramatically cheaper than waiting, and waiting too long can mean you're turned down entirely.

3. A hybrid life insurance policy with a long-term care rider. This is the option I recommend most often, because it solves the "what if I never need care" objection: if you need long-term care, the policy pays out toward it; if you don't, your family still receives a life insurance death benefit. Nothing is wasted either way.

4. Nevada Medicaid. Once countable assets are spent down to Nevada's $2,000 limit for a single applicant, Medicaid's Home and Community-Based Services waiver and nursing facility coverage can take over. Nevada uses a 60-month look-back period on asset transfers, and a spouse who isn't applying can typically keep up to $162,660 in assets under the Community Spouse Resource Allowance. Medicaid planning is powerful, but it needs to start well before a crisis — transfers made inside that 60-month window can trigger a penalty period.

What's different about planning for this in Northern Nevada

Where you live changes the plan. In Reno and Carson City, there's a reasonable supply of assisted living communities and home care agencies to compare. In Minden and Gardnerville and around Lake Tahoe, options are thinner and families more often lean on in-home care or plan to relocate a parent closer to family. In Dayton, the nearest full-service facilities are typically a drive into Carson City or Reno, which is worth factoring into a care plan long before it's needed. None of this shows up in a national cost estimate — it only shows up when someone who knows the area walks through it with you.

It's also worth reading together with the Medicare decision itself: choosing between Medicare Advantage and Medicare Supplement affects which doctors and hospitals are in-network if a hospital stay ever triggers that 100-day skilled nursing benefit — one more reason these two decisions are easier to make in the same conversation, not two separate ones.

How to actually start

1. Get honest about your family history and health — that shapes both urgency and which products you'll qualify for. 2. Decide, as a family, whether the goal is protecting a spouse's income, preserving an inheritance, or simply avoiding being a burden on your kids — the right product often follows from the goal. 3. Compare traditional long-term care insurance against a hybrid life/LTC policy side by side, in real numbers. 4. If Medicaid is part of the plan, start the conversation years before it's needed, not after a diagnosis.

That's the conversation I have with Northern Nevada families every week — no pressure, and no cost to sit down and go through it.

Good questions

Frequently asked.

Does Medicare pay for long-term care in Nevada?

Not for custodial care. Medicare covers up to 100 days in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying 3-day hospital stay — the first 20 days at no cost, days 21-100 with a daily coinsurance ($217/day in 2026) — then nothing. It does not cover ongoing help with bathing, dressing, or meals at home or in assisted living, which is what most long-term care actually is.

How much does a long-term care insurance policy cost?

It depends heavily on your age and health at the time you apply. Buying in your mid-50s to early 60s, while you're healthy, is typically far more affordable than waiting — and waiting too long can mean you no longer qualify at all. A hybrid life insurance policy with a long-term care rider is often a better fit for Nevadans who want a benefit even if they never need care.

Can I qualify for Nevada Medicaid and keep my house?

Often yes, at least at first — a primary home is typically an exempt asset up to a home equity limit while you or your spouse live there, though Nevada's Medicaid Estate Recovery Program can seek reimbursement from the estate after both spouses have passed. This is exactly the kind of detail worth reviewing with a professional before you need care, not after.

What's the difference between assisted living and a nursing home in cost terms?

In Nevada, assisted living generally runs several thousand dollars less per month than a nursing home, because assisted living provides help with daily activities while a nursing home also provides ongoing medical/skilled nursing care. In-home care is billed hourly and can cost less than facility care at part-time hours, but often costs more once a family needs care most of the day.
