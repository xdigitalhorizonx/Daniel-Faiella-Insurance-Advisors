Annuities, explained without the jargon.

How fixed and indexed annuities actually work, when one fits a Nevada retirement plan, and the trade-offs nobody mentions until you're already signing.

Get a free retirement income review →

At some point in retirement planning, almost every Nevadan runs into the same question: what happens when the paychecks stop and it's just Social Security, savings, and whatever Medicare doesn't cover? An annuity is one of the few financial tools built specifically to answer that question — it's a contract with an insurance company that turns a lump sum into a guaranteed income stream, for a set number of years or for the rest of your life. Used well, it's a paycheck replacement. Used poorly, it's an expensive, illiquid mistake. Here's the honest version, without the sales pitch.

Fixed vs. indexed: the two annuities most retirees actually consider

There are several kinds of annuities, but for retirement income planning, two do almost all of the work:

 Fixed AnnuityFixed Indexed Annuity
How interest is creditedA set rate the insurer guarantees for a term, much like a CD.Tied to a market index (like the S&P 500), credited only when the index rises.
Downside protectionPrincipal never loses value from market moves.A floor (commonly 0%) means a down market year credits no interest — but no loss either.
Upside potentialCapped at the guaranteed rate — no market participation.Capped by a rate cap or participation rate, so you get a share of gains, not all of them.
Best forPredictability lovers who want a CD-like guarantee with tax deferral.Retirees who want some growth potential without market risk to principal.
ComplexitySimple — one rate, one term.More moving parts: caps, participation rates, index crediting methods.

What an annuity is actually good for

The strongest case for an annuity isn't "beating the market" — it's filling an income gap. If Social Security and any pension don't cover your fixed monthly expenses, an income annuity (or an indexed annuity with a guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit) can close that gap with a payment that continues no matter how long you live or how the market behaves. That's a form of insurance most people don't think of as insurance: longevity insurance. It also plays a quiet role in Medicare and long-term care planning, since a predictable income floor makes it easier to budget for premiums and out-of-pocket costs that arrive on their own schedule.

The trade-offs nobody mentions upfront

Annuities aren't free lunches, and a good advisor should say so before you buy, not after:

  • Liquidity is limited. Most contracts allow a free annual withdrawal (often 10%), but pulling out more during the surrender period triggers a surrender charge that can run into the double digits in year one, declining each year after.
  • Caps and participation rates move. An indexed annuity's cap can be reset by the insurer at each contract anniversary, within the bounds the contract allows — read the renewal provisions, not just the year-one illustration.
  • Riders cost money. A guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit or an enhanced death benefit is valuable, but it's an added fee layered on top of the base contract — make sure you're actually going to use it.
  • It's not the place for your emergency fund. Keep several months of expenses in cash outside any annuity before committing longer-term savings.

Why Nevada changes the math

Nevada has no state income tax, which matters more for annuities than most products: the earnings inside a nonqualified annuity are tax-deferred until withdrawal, and when you do take income, Nevada won't add its own tax on top of the federal bill. That's a meaningful advantage for retirees relocating from higher-tax states to Reno, Carson City, or the Carson Valley, and it's part of why retirement income and annuities are a growing conversation across Northern Nevada. Nevada also requires that any annuity recommendation meet a best-interest standard and gives you a free-look period after purchase to cancel for a full refund if it isn't right — protections worth knowing you have, whether you buy through us or anyone else.

How to tell if one fits your plan

Start with the gap, not the product: list your guaranteed income (Social Security, pension) against your fixed monthly expenses. If there's a shortfall, that's the number an income annuity is sized to close — not your entire nest egg. From there, weigh how much of the remaining balance you can afford to lock up for 5–10 years in exchange for guarantees, versus how much you want fully liquid. That comparison, done in real numbers rather than a sales illustration, is the free review I walk Northern Nevada retirees through every week.

Good questions

Frequently asked.

Is an annuity a good idea for retirement?

It can be, for the right piece of your plan. An annuity is best used to guarantee a stream of income you can't outlive or to protect a portion of savings from market swings — not as a place for every dollar. Whether it's a good idea depends on your other income sources, your liquidity needs, and the specific contract's costs and terms.

What's the difference between a fixed annuity and an indexed annuity?

A fixed annuity credits a set interest rate the insurer guarantees for a period, similar to a CD. An indexed annuity credits interest based on the performance of a market index, usually with a 0% floor so you can't lose principal to a market downturn, but with a cap or participation rate that limits how much upside you capture.

Can I lose money in an indexed annuity?

Your principal is protected from market loss by the contract's floor, typically 0%, so a down market year simply credits little or no interest rather than a loss. You can still come out behind if you surrender the contract early and pay a surrender charge, or if fees erode value, so read the specific contract terms.

Do I pay Nevada state tax on annuity income?

Nevada has no state income tax, so annuity income is not taxed at the state level. The earnings portion of a nonqualified annuity is still subject to federal ordinary income tax when withdrawn, and early withdrawals before 59½ can trigger a federal penalty on the earnings.

What if I need my money back early?

Most annuities carry a surrender charge schedule, often declining over several years, if you withdraw more than the contract's free annual withdrawal amount during that period. Nevada also requires a free-look period after you buy, during which you can cancel for a full refund if the contract isn't right for you.
