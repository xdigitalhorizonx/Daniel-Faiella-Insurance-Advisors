Long-Term Care · Nevada · 2026

Who pays when you need care at home?

Medicare gaps, the real cost of assisted living and in-home care in Nevada, and the options that protect a lifetime of savings.

Get a free long-term care review →

Ask most Nevadans whether Medicare will cover a nursing home if they ever need one, and the honest answer catches them off guard: almost never. Medicare is health insurance, built to treat illness and injury — it was never designed to pay for the custodial care most people picture when they say "long-term care": help getting dressed, bathing, eating, or simply being supervised safely at home or in a facility. That gap between what Medicare pays and what care actually costs is quietly one of the largest unplanned expenses in Nevada retirements. Here's what Medicare really covers, what long-term care costs in Nevada today, and what actually fills the gap.

What Medicare actually covers — and where it stops

Medicare Part A covers up to 100 days of care in a skilled nursing facility per benefit period, but only after a qualifying 3-day inpatient hospital stay, and only for as long as a doctor certifies you need daily skilled care — physical therapy after a hip replacement, for example, not ongoing help with daily living. In 2026, you pay $0 per day for days 1–20 after meeting the $1,736 Part A deductible, then $217 per day for days 21–100. After day 100 in that benefit period, Medicare pays nothing — Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage plans alike. There is no long-term custodial care benefit to renew or extend. If what you need is ongoing help rather than short-term rehabilitation, Medicare simply isn't the tool for it, regardless of which Medicare Advantage or Supplement plan you carry.

Why this hits Northern Nevada families especially hard

Care access is uneven across our service area. Reno and Carson City have the region's deepest bench of skilled nursing and assisted living facilities, largely tied to the Renown and Carson Tahoe Health systems. In the Carson Valley, Carson Valley Health anchors care in Minden and Gardnerville, but options thin out fast. Around Lake Tahoe, Barton Health covers acute needs, yet long-term placements often mean a move to Carson City or Reno — away from a spouse, familiar surroundings, and a support network. And in Dayton and Fernley, there's essentially no local long-term care facility at all, so families either drive long distances for visits or bring care into the home at a higher hourly cost. If you've retired to Nevada precisely because there's no state income tax, it's worth knowing that advantage doesn't offset a long-term care bill — that has to be planned for separately.

What actually pays for long-term care

Once it's clear Medicare isn't the answer, families in Northern Nevada generally draw from some combination of:

  • Medicaid — will pay for long-term care, but only after you've spent down assets to Nevada's eligibility limits, and it comes with a five-year look-back on transfers. It's a real safety net, not a plan.
  • Standalone long-term care insurance — pays a daily or monthly benefit toward care, but premiums rise with age and health, and if you never use it, you never see the money back.
  • Hybrid life insurance with a long-term care or chronic-illness rider — an increasingly popular middle path. It pays a long-term care benefit if you need one, or a death benefit to your family if you don't, so the premium is never "wasted."
  • Annuities with an enhanced long-term-care withdrawal benefit — for retirees already using an annuity for retirement income, some contracts add a rider that increases the payout if you need qualified long-term care, without a separate policy.
  • Self-funding — viable for some, but it means earmarking a large, liquid sum that can't also be counted on for everyday retirement income.

How to actually plan for it

1. Get honest about the real cost for the type of care you'd likely want — in-home, assisted living, or skilled nursing — rather than budgeting off a national average. 2. Check what you already have — some life insurance and annuity contracts already include riders worth revisiting before buying something new. 3. Plan early: underwriting for long-term care and hybrid life products gets harder and more expensive with every year you wait, and a health event can close the door on traditional coverage entirely. 4. Coordinate it with the rest of the plan — your Medicare coverage, your retirement income, and your long-term care funding should be reviewed together, not as three separate decisions.

Good questions

Frequently asked.

Does Medicare cover nursing home care in Nevada?

Only short-term. Medicare Part A pays for up to 100 days in a skilled nursing facility per benefit period, but only after a qualifying 3-day hospital stay and only while you need daily skilled care. It does not cover long-term custodial care — help with bathing, dressing, eating, or supervision — which is what most nursing home and assisted living stays actually are.

How much does long-term care cost in Nevada?

Based on the most recent CareScout/Genworth Cost of Care Survey figures for Nevada, assisted living runs a median of roughly $6,110 a month, a semi-private nursing home room about $11,790 a month, a private nursing home room about $14,460 a month, and a home health aide around $7,245 a month at 44 hours a week. Costs vary by city and level of care.

What actually pays for long-term care if Medicare doesn't?

Four main paths: Medicaid, once you've spent down assets to Nevada's eligibility limits; a standalone long-term care insurance policy; a hybrid life insurance policy with a long-term care or chronic-illness rider, which pays a benefit whether or not you ever need care; an annuity with an enhanced long-term-care withdrawal benefit; or simply self-funding from savings. Most families end up using a combination.

When should I start planning for long-term care coverage?

Earlier than most people think — ideally in your 50s or early 60s, while you're still healthy enough to qualify for underwriting and premiums are lower. Waiting until after a diagnosis or a fall often means you can no longer qualify for traditional coverage at any price.
