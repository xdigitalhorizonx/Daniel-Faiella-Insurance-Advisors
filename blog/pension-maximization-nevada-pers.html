Retirement Income

The PERS choice you only get to make once.

Electing a survivor option quietly shrinks your pension check every month for the rest of your life. Sometimes a life insurance policy can protect your spouse for less — and sometimes it's a costly mistake. Here's how to tell which one you're looking at.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · October 26, 2026 · 7 min read

If you've spent your career with the State of Nevada, a county, a city, a school district, or any other Nevada PERS employer, the biggest financial decision of your retirement isn't how you invest — it's a single box you check on your retirement application. PERS asks you to choose how your monthly benefit is paid, and once your payments begin, that choice is permanent. There's no do-over.

The decision comes down to a trade almost nobody frames clearly: take the largest possible check for your own life, or accept a smaller check for life so that your spouse keeps receiving something after you're gone. "Pension maximization" is a strategy that tries to give you both — the bigger check and protection for your spouse — by using life insurance to do the job the survivor option would have done. It's a genuinely smart idea for some retirees and a genuinely bad one for others, and the difference is worth understanding before you sign.

1. The PERS election, in plain terms

At retirement, PERS generally lets you choose between the unmodified allowance — the highest monthly amount, paid for your lifetime and ending at your death — and several survivor options that pay you a reduced amount each month in exchange for continuing a lifetime benefit to a beneficiary you name, usually a spouse. The size of the reduction, and how much your survivor would ultimately receive, depend on which option you pick and on both of your ages.

This matters more for public employees than most people realize, because many Nevada PERS members don't pay into Social Security through their government job. That means the usual Social Security survivor benefit a private-sector widow or widower might fall back on may be small or absent — so the PERS survivor election can be the main thing standing between your spouse and a sharp drop in income. The stakes are high, and the decision is one-way. Confirm your exact numbers directly with PERS before you do anything else.

2. What pension maximization actually proposes

Here's the logic. The survivor option isn't free — you "pay" for it every month through a permanently reduced benefit. Pension maximization asks a simple question: what if you took the full unmodified allowance, and used part of that extra monthly income to buy a life insurance policy on yourself instead?

If the plan works, you collect the bigger check for as long as you live. If you die first, your spouse receives a tax-free life insurance death benefit they can use to replace the pension income you were providing — or convert into lifetime income of their own through something like an income annuity. And if your spouse dies before you, you keep the full pension and can stop or repurpose the coverage — flexibility a locked-in survivor election never gives you.

3. When the math actually favors it

Pension maximization is a comparison, not a product, and it only comes out ahead when several things line up at once:

  • You're in good health and insurable. The whole strategy depends on buying coverage for less than the survivor option costs you. If your health makes insurance expensive — or impossible to get — the comparison collapses before it starts.
  • The reduction is meaningful. The wider the gap between the unmodified allowance and the survivor option, the more room there is to fund a policy and still come out ahead.
  • You buy the right kind and amount of coverage. The policy has to be large enough to truly replace the benefit and permanent enough to last as long as your spouse might — not a term policy that expires at 65 while your spouse lives to 92.
  • You'll actually keep the policy in force. Miss premiums and let it lapse, and you've given up the survivor option for nothing. The discipline is part of the plan.

Sizing that policy correctly is the heart of the whole exercise — the same discipline behind figuring out how much life insurance you actually need at any stage of life, applied to a very specific job: standing in for a pension.

4. The honest risks — where it goes wrong

I won't pretend this strategy is for everyone, because it isn't, and it's been oversold plenty of times. Weigh these before anyone shows you an illustration:

 PERS survivor optionPension maximization
Spouse's protectionGuaranteed lifetime benefit from PERS.Depends on the policy staying in force and being large enough.
InflationMay include PERS post-retirement increases.A fixed death benefit doesn't automatically grow with inflation.

The quiet killer on that list is cost-of-living increases. If your PERS survivor benefit would grow over time and your insurance death benefit is a flat number, the pension can pull ahead over a long widowhood. A good analysis accounts for that, rather than comparing today's dollars to today's dollars and calling it a day.

5. How to do it the right way

If the numbers and your health line up, the execution matters as much as the decision. Lock in the life insurance before you finalize your PERS election — you never want to waive the survivor option and then discover you can't qualify for coverage. Favor permanent coverage designed to last your spouse's lifetime over a term policy that could expire first, and make sure the death benefit is sized to generate the income the pension would have. Then coordinate the rest: how the proceeds would be invested or annuitized, and — just as important — confirming that your beneficiary designations on the policy and your other accounts actually match your wishes, since those forms override your will. This is as much an estate decision as an income one.

6. Why the Nevada picture is a little friendlier

Two things tilt the math gently in a Nevada retiree's favor. First, Nevada has no state income tax, so your PERS benefit isn't taxed by the state and neither is the income your spouse would draw from insurance proceeds — one less layer on both sides of the comparison. Second, a life insurance death benefit generally passes to your spouse income-tax-free anywhere, so you're weighing a taxable pension stream against a tax-free lump sum. Neither fact makes pension maximization automatically right — the health and longevity questions still decide that — but for retirees settling in Carson City, Reno, or the Carson Valley, it's a real point in the plus column. It also fits naturally into the broader question of whether you still need life insurance in retirement at all.

Good questions

Pension maximization questions, answered straight.

What is pension maximization?

Pension maximization is a retirement-income strategy where you elect the highest single-life pension payout — in Nevada PERS, the "unmodified" allowance that stops when you die — instead of a reduced survivor option, and use the extra monthly income to pay for a life insurance policy that protects your spouse. If you pass away first, your spouse receives the insurance death benefit in place of a continuing pension. It only works well when you can qualify for affordable coverage and the policy is large and permanent enough to replace the survivor benefit for your spouse's whole life.

How do Nevada PERS survivor options work?

At retirement, PERS lets you choose the unmodified allowance, which pays the most each month but generally ends at your death, or one of several options that pay a reduced monthly amount in exchange for continuing a lifetime benefit to a named survivor. The reduction and exactly how much your beneficiary would receive depend on the option you pick and both of your ages. Because the election is permanent once benefits begin, confirm your exact figures directly with PERS before you decide.

Is pension maximization a good idea for Nevada public employees?

Sometimes — and sometimes it's a costly mistake. It can work when you're in good health and insurable, the gap between the unmodified and survivor options is wide, and you buy permanent coverage sized to truly replace the survivor benefit. It tends to fail when your health makes insurance expensive or unavailable, when the survivor option carries cost-of-living increases the insurance won't match, or when a term policy lapses or expires before your spouse does. It's a math-and-health question, not a product pitch, so run the real numbers before electing.

Does Nevada tax my PERS pension or a life insurance death benefit?

Nevada has no state income tax, so your PERS benefit isn't taxed at the state level, though it's generally taxable federally. A life insurance death benefit is generally income-tax-free to your beneficiary in every state. That combination can make the after-tax comparison between a survivor pension and an insurance death benefit a little cleaner for Nevada retirees, but taxes are only one piece — the health and longevity math matters more.
