Medicare Costs

The Medicare surcharge nobody warns you about.

IRMAA can quietly add hundreds of dollars a month to your Part B and Part D premiums — triggered by a tax return from two years ago. Here's how it works, what actually counts as income, and how to plan around it.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · August 20, 2026 · 8 min read

A Minden couple I work with sold a rental property two years ago — a good year, financially. This spring, their Medicare Part B premium jumped by more than $100 a month each, and neither of them had any idea why until we pulled up the notice together. That's IRMAA: the Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount, a surcharge that Social Security adds to Medicare premiums based on income from a tax return filed two years earlier. It isn't rare, and it isn't reserved for the ultra-wealthy — a home sale, a large Roth conversion, or a required minimum distribution can push an ordinary retiree over a threshold they never saw coming.

What IRMAA actually is — and isn't

IRMAA isn't a penalty, a fine, or anything punitive. It's simply a higher premium bracket. Everyone on Medicare pays the same base amount for Part B and, separately, for a Part D drug plan — but if your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) rises above certain thresholds, Social Security moves you into a higher tier and adds a surcharge to both. The surcharge is billed automatically; you don't apply for it, and most people don't hear about it until the notice arrives.

The part that catches Nevada retirees off guard is the two-year look-back. Your 2026 Medicare premium is based on the MAGI reported on your 2024 tax return — the most recent complete return the IRS has on file when Social Security sets next year's premiums each fall. So a high-income event this year won't touch your premium this year. It shows up on your Medicare bill roughly two years from now, often long after you've forgotten what caused it.

The IRMAA brackets, in plain terms

Social Security groups income into five surcharge tiers above the standard premium, applied separately to Part B and Part D. The most recently published amounts (2025) look like this:

The income that catches people off guard

MAGI for IRMAA purposes is broader than most people expect — it adds back tax-exempt municipal bond interest to your adjusted gross income. In my Northern Nevada practice, four things trigger surprise IRMAA letters more than anything else:

  • Selling a second home or rental property. Reno and Tahoe real estate has appreciated enough that even after the home-sale exclusion, a large capital gain is common — and it counts in full toward MAGI the year of the sale.
  • Roth conversions. Converting a big chunk of a traditional IRA in one year is often smart tax strategy long-term, but it's also ordinary income in the year you convert — and can push you into a surcharge tier you'd otherwise avoid.
  • Required minimum distributions (RMDs). Once RMDs begin, they're forced income whether you need the cash or not, and they only grow as a percentage of your account balance with age.
  • A one-time annuity surrender or large withdrawal. Cashing out more of a deferred annuity than planned in a single year adds a lump of taxable income exactly like an RMD does.

How to plan around it

Because IRMAA is based on a two-year-old tax return, the window to manage it is well before the bill ever arrives:

  • Spread large conversions over several years instead of one, keeping each year's MAGI below the next threshold when it's practical to do so.
  • Use a Qualified Charitable Distribution (QCD) to satisfy some or all of an RMD directly from an IRA to a charity — the amount never touches your MAGI at all.
  • Time large asset sales deliberately, especially around a year you're also taking a big distribution, so the two don't stack in the same tax year.
  • Structure annuity income for steady, predictable payouts rather than large irregular withdrawals — this is exactly the kind of planning I do with clients comparing annuity income strategies against RMD timing.

And if a life-changing event has already lowered your income since the year that triggered the surcharge — retirement, reduced hours, divorce, or the death of a spouse — you can file Form SSA-44 with Social Security to have your premium recalculated using more current income instead of the two-year-old figure. Many Nevadans who qualify for this never file it, simply because they don't know it exists.

Who should be watching this in Northern Nevada

Anyone approaching or already on Medicare who's also managing a taxable event: a Tahoe or Reno property sale, a Roth conversion strategy, a business sale, or the start of RMDs at 73. It's worth a look two to three years before you plan any of those events — not after the IRMAA letter shows up — since the premium impact lags the income by two full years and there's rarely anything to do retroactively once it arrives.

Good questions

IRMAA questions, answered straight.

What is IRMAA on Medicare?

IRMAA stands for Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount. It's a surcharge added to your Medicare Part B and Part D premiums when your modified adjusted gross income rises above certain thresholds. It isn't a fine or a penalty — it's simply a higher premium tier, and it's added automatically by Social Security based on your tax return.

How far back does Medicare look at my income for IRMAA?

Two years. Your 2026 premium is based on the modified adjusted gross income reported on your 2024 tax return, the most recent complete return the IRS has on file when Social Security sets premiums for the year. That two-year lag is exactly why a one-time high-income event can catch retirees off guard well after the year that caused it.

Can I appeal an IRMAA surcharge?

Yes, in specific circumstances. If your income has dropped since the tax year used to calculate your surcharge because of a life-changing event — retirement, reduced work hours, marriage, divorce, death of a spouse, or loss of income-producing property — you can file Form SSA-44 with Social Security to have your premium recalculated using more current income instead of the two-year-old figure.

Does IRMAA apply to Medicare Advantage plans?

Indirectly, yes. IRMAA is a surcharge on Part B and, separately, on Part D drug coverage — not on the Medicare Advantage plan premium itself. But because most Medicare Advantage plans include Part B and bundle in Part D drug coverage, an IRMAA surcharge shows up as an add-on to your monthly bill even if your plan's base premium is $0.
