Medicare IRMAA: the high-income surcharge that surprises retirees.

A letter arrives from Social Security saying your Medicare premium is going up — sometimes doubling or tripling. Here's what IRMAA is, the 2026 income brackets, and how Nevada retirees plan around it.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · September 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Most people meet IRMAA by mail. You're a year or two into Medicare, your income has been steady, and then Social Security sends a notice that your Part B premium is jumping — not by a few dollars, but by hundreds a month. There's no new benefit, no change in your coverage, just a bigger bill. It feels like a mistake. It usually isn't.

IRMAA — the Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount — is the surcharge Medicare adds to your Part B and Part D premiums when your income is above a threshold. It catches Nevada retirees off guard more than almost any other Medicare rule, because it's driven by a tax return you filed two years ago and by income events most people don't connect to their Medicare premium. Here's how it actually works, the 2026 numbers, and what you can do about it.

What IRMAA actually is

Everyone on Medicare pays a Part B premium — $202.90 a month in 2026 for most people. If your income is above the IRMAA threshold, you pay that base amount plus a surcharge, on both Part B and your Part D drug coverage. It isn't a tax you file; Social Security calculates it automatically and deducts it from your benefit (or bills you directly). Only higher-income beneficiaries — roughly the top 8% — pay it at all, but for those who do, it can more than triple the standard premium. It applies whether you have Original Medicare with a Medicare Supplement or a Medicare Advantage plan; IRMAA rides on Part B and Part D no matter which path you chose.

The two-year lookback that trips people up

This is the part almost nobody expects. Your IRMAA isn't based on this year's income — it's based on the modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) from your tax return two years earlier. So your 2026 premiums are set by the income on your 2024 return. MAGI for this purpose is simply your adjusted gross income plus any tax-exempt interest (municipal bond interest counts, even though it's not taxed).

That lag is why a single good year can surface as a surprise premium two years later — long after you've forgotten about it. A one-time event in 2024 shows up on your 2026 Medicare bill, and the events that do it are exactly the ones common in retirement.

Here's what the 2026 surcharge looks like, based on your 2024 MAGI. The Part B figure is the total monthly premium — standard base plus surcharge — per person.

On top of those Part B amounts, each IRMAA tier adds a Part D surcharge — running from about $14.50 up to roughly $91.00 a month in 2026 — paid on top of whatever your drug plan charges. Married couples where both spouses are on Medicare pay the surcharge each, so a joint return over a threshold effectively doubles the hit.

The cliff that makes it sting

IRMAA is not phased in gradually — it's a set of cliffs. Go one dollar over a bracket and you owe the entire surcharge for that tier, not a prorated slice of it. A couple whose 2024 MAGI landed at $218,001 pays the same higher premium as one at $274,000. That single-dollar sensitivity is what makes IRMAA a planning problem rather than just a fact of life: the difference between staying under a line and crossing it can be hundreds or thousands of dollars over the year, for both spouses.

Why Nevada retirees cross the line without meaning to

Nevada has no state income tax, which is a genuine advantage — but it does nothing for IRMAA, because IRMAA keys off federal MAGI. The income events that push Northern Nevada retirees over a threshold are usually one-time and predictable:

  • Required minimum distributions. Once RMDs begin, forced IRA withdrawals can lift MAGI more than people expect — one reason some retirees use an annuity strategy to manage the RMDs on their IRA.
  • Roth conversions. Converting a traditional IRA to a Roth is smart planning, but the converted amount counts as income the year you do it — and can trip IRMAA two years later if it's done in one large chunk.
  • Selling a home. With how far home values have climbed in Reno, Carson City, and around Lake Tahoe, the capital gain above the exclusion on a long-held house can single-handedly launch you into a higher bracket.
  • Still earning at 65-plus. Wages or business income while you're working past 65 and on Medicare stack on top of everything else in your MAGI.

None of these are mistakes. They're normal retirement moves — they just have a Medicare-premium side effect two years down the road that's easy to miss.

If you got the letter: how to appeal

If your income has since dropped — and for most new retirees it has — you don't have to accept a surcharge based on a two-year-old, higher figure. Social Security lets you request a recalculation for a qualifying life-changing event using Form SSA-44. The events that count include:

  • Work stoppage or reduction — retirement itself qualifies, and it's the most common reason a recent retiree's IRMAA can be waved off;
  • Marriage, divorce or annulment, or the death of a spouse;
  • Loss of a pension, or loss of income-producing property through no fault of your own.

You file SSA-44 with proof of the event and your estimated lower income, and Social Security recalculates using the more recent figure. Separately, if the IRS data was simply wrong or you later amended the return, you can ask for a correction. Don't just pay a surcharge that no longer reflects your reality — it's often reversible.

Planning around it before it happens

The better move is to see IRMAA coming. Because it's recalculated every year against your latest return, a one-time spike affects roughly one year of premiums and then falls away — so the goal is usually to keep any single year from crossing a bracket you didn't need to. A few levers matter:

  • Spread income across years. A Roth conversion or a large withdrawal split over two or three years may stay under the threshold each year instead of blowing through it once.
  • Mind the two-year runway. The year you plan a big income event is the year that sets a Medicare premium two years later — plan it with that calendar in mind.
  • Coordinate the whole picture. RMDs, Social Security timing, capital gains, and Medicare premiums are one connected system. On the other end of the income scale, lower-income Nevadans should check whether they qualify for Extra Help and Medicare Savings Programs — the mirror image of IRMAA.

Timing your enrollment matters too; if you're new to all of this, start with how Medicare's enrollment periods work in Nevada so a missed deadline doesn't compound an IRMAA surprise.

The bottom line

IRMAA isn't a penalty for doing something wrong — it's a predictable consequence of income that most people simply never had explained to them. Understand the two-year lookback, know where the 2026 brackets sit, and either time your income around them or appeal when a life change has already lowered it. Good questions

IRMAA questions, answered straight.

What is Medicare IRMAA and who has to pay it?

IRMAA — the Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount — is a surcharge added to your Medicare Part B and Part D premiums when your income is above the annual threshold. In 2026 it applies to individuals with modified adjusted gross income above $109,000, or married couples filing jointly above $218,000. Roughly the top 8 percent of Medicare beneficiaries pay it. Everyone below those thresholds pays only the standard Part B premium of $202.90 a month with no Part D surcharge.

What income is used to calculate my 2026 IRMAA?

Social Security uses your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) from your tax return two years earlier, so your 2026 IRMAA is based on the income you reported on your 2024 federal return. MAGI for IRMAA is your adjusted gross income plus any tax-exempt interest. Because of that two-year lookback, a one-time income spike in 2024 — a large IRA withdrawal, a Roth conversion, or the capital gain from selling a home — can raise your Medicare premiums in 2026.

Can I appeal or reduce my IRMAA?

Yes. If your income has dropped because of a qualifying life-changing event — retirement or work reduction, marriage, divorce, the death of a spouse, or loss of a pension or income-producing property — you can file Form SSA-44 with Social Security to have IRMAA recalculated on your more recent, lower income instead of the two-year-old figure. You can also request a correction if the IRS data used was wrong or amended.

Does a one-time income spike raise my Medicare premiums forever?

No. IRMAA is recalculated every year against the most recent tax return on file, so a single high-income year generally affects only about one year of premiums two years later. Once your income returns to a lower bracket, your surcharge falls away automatically the following year. That is why timing income — spreading a Roth conversion or a large withdrawal across years — can keep a one-time event from pushing you over a threshold.
