Life Insurance

What happens to your life insurance in a Nevada divorce?

Divorce reshuffles nearly every part of your financial life — and life insurance is one of the pieces people forget until it causes a problem. Here's how Nevada law actually treats your beneficiaries, your policy, and the coverage a decree can require.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · November 23, 2026 · 7 min read

When a marriage ends, the attention goes where you'd expect — the house, the retirement accounts, custody, the monthly budget on one income instead of two. Life insurance tends to sit quietly in the background until, months or years later, it surprises someone. A death benefit pays out to an ex-spouse nobody meant to keep as beneficiary. A decree requires coverage that was never actually put in place. A newly single parent discovers the old policy was sized for a household that no longer exists.

None of that has to happen. In a Nevada divorce, life insurance comes down to three questions: who gets the money if you die, whether the policy itself is a marital asset, and whether the court will require you to carry coverage. Handle those three and you've handled almost everything.

Your beneficiary may not update the way you expect

This is where most of the trouble lives. People assume a divorce automatically erases their ex as beneficiary, or they assume it never does. In Nevada, the reality is in between — and the exception is the part that bites.

Under Nevada law (NRS 111.781), once a divorce or annulment is final, a revocable beneficiary designation naming your former spouse on an individual policy you own is generally revoked by operation of law, as if they had died before you, unless the policy or a court order says otherwise. That's a helpful backstop — but it is the worst possible thing to rely on, for two reasons.

First, it does not apply to employer group life insurance. Coverage through your job is usually governed by federal ERISA law, which overrides the state statute, so the plan pays whoever is named on file — even an ex-spouse — regardless of the Nevada rule. The U.S. Supreme Court has upheld exactly that result, and it's a recurring, avoidable tragedy. Second, even for individual policies, depending on an automatic revocation invites disputes, delays, and claims that can take an estate to court. The fix is simple and permanent: once your divorce is final, submit a fresh beneficiary form to every carrier — individual policies and your employer plan alike. Treat it as a checklist item, not a legal theory. The same principle runs through your beneficiary designations on retirement and estate accounts, which quietly override your will no matter what it says.

One important timing note: you generally cannot change beneficiaries while the divorce is still pending. When a case is filed in Nevada, a Joint Preliminary Injunction typically takes effect that freezes insurance changes for both spouses until the decree is entered. So the sequence is: leave it alone while the case is open, then update it promptly once the decree is final — within the bounds of whatever the decree requires.

Is the policy itself a marital asset?

Nevada is a community-property state, which means most assets and debts acquired during the marriage are owned equally and divided accordingly. Whether your life insurance is part of that split depends entirely on the kind of policy you have.

Term life insurance — pure protection with no cash value — is generally not treated as a divisible asset, because there's nothing to divide beyond the question of who keeps paying the premium. Permanent policies are different. A whole life or indexed universal life (IUL) policy that built up cash value during the marriage can be community property in Nevada, and that cash value may be split, offset against other assets, or assigned to one spouse who keeps the policy. If you own permanent coverage, flag it early so it's valued correctly instead of overlooked — it's real money, and it's easy to miss on a balance sheet full of houses and 401(k)s.

When the decree requires you to carry coverage

Life insurance isn't just something a divorce affects — it's often something a divorce requires. Nevada courts frequently order the paying spouse to maintain a policy so that alimony or child support survives their death. The logic is straightforward: if the person paying support dies, the support stops, and the children or ex-spouse who counted on it are left exposed. A life insurance requirement in the decree closes that gap.

If your decree includes one of these provisions, the details matter. Make sure the policy's death benefit amount, ownership, and beneficiary line up exactly with what the order says — commonly the children or the receiving spouse named as an irrevocable beneficiary so the coverage can't be quietly redirected later. Keep proof the policy is in force. And if you need to buy new coverage to satisfy the order, arrange it before the ink dries rather than assuming an old employer policy — which disappears the day you leave that job — will do the job. For families already thinking about how support and assets flow after a death, a living trust can add another layer of control over how and when children receive money.

If you're newly single, re-run your own number

Here's the piece almost everyone skips. The coverage you bought as part of a two-income, two-parent household is probably the wrong size now. Maybe you need more — you're the sole provider for your kids, and there's no second income to fall back on if something happens to you. Maybe you need less — the mortgage was refinanced away or the kids are nearly grown. Either way, the honest move after a divorce is to recalculate from scratch using your new reality. My walkthrough on how much life insurance you actually need lays out the DIME method for exactly this.

And don't stop at the death benefit. As a newly single earner, your paycheck is the engine behind everything — which is why income protection if you can't work deserves a look at the same time. If you're the one receiving support, the mirror-image question applies: your financial security now depends on an ex-spouse's income, so making sure there's coverage on their life — ideally baked into the decree — protects the payments you're counting on.

A short divorce-and-life-insurance checklist

  • While the case is open: don't change beneficiaries; confirm what the Joint Preliminary Injunction allows.
  • Read the decree closely for any life insurance requirement — amount, owner, beneficiary, and duration.
  • Once it's final: file new beneficiary forms with every carrier, including your employer plan.
  • Value permanent policies (whole life, IUL) as community property if cash value grew during the marriage.
  • Re-size your own coverage to your post-divorce household and budget.
  • Protect your support — secure coverage on the paying spouse if you rely on alimony or child support.

Good questions

Life insurance and divorce, answered straight.

Does divorce automatically remove my ex as beneficiary in Nevada?

For an individual policy you own, Nevada law (NRS 111.781) generally revokes a revocable beneficiary designation in favor of a former spouse once the divorce is final, unless the policy or a court order says otherwise. But never rely on that alone — it does not apply to employer group life insurance governed by federal ERISA law, and the cleanest fix is to submit a new beneficiary form to your carrier yourself once the divorce is complete.

Can I change my beneficiary while the divorce is pending?

Usually not. When a Nevada divorce is filed, a Joint Preliminary Injunction typically takes effect that prohibits both spouses from changing beneficiaries, canceling, or transferring insurance while the case is open. Beneficiary changes generally wait until the decree is entered, and the decree itself may dictate who stays on the policy and for how long. Ask your attorney before making any change.

Is my life insurance policy split in a Nevada divorce?

It depends on the type. Term life usually has no cash value and is generally not a divisible asset, though who continues to pay for it can still be addressed. A permanent policy — whole life or IUL — that built cash value during the marriage can be community property in Nevada and subject to division, which may mean splitting the cash value, offsetting it against other assets, or one spouse keeping the policy.

My decree says I must keep life insurance — what do I do?

Nevada courts often order the paying spouse to maintain a policy to secure alimony or child support. Make sure the policy's death benefit, ownership, and beneficiary match exactly what the decree requires — often the children or the receiving spouse named as an irrevocable beneficiary — and keep proof of coverage. If you need a new or replacement policy to satisfy the order, line it up before relying on an old one that may have lapsed or changed.

Should I buy a policy on my ex-spouse?

If you depend on alimony or child support, insuring the paying ex-spouse's life can protect those payments if they pass away. The simplest approach is for the decree to require the paying spouse to carry coverage with you or the children as beneficiary. Where that isn't in place, a spouse who receives support may be able to own a policy on the ex, with their cooperation, since you have an insurable interest in the income you rely on.
