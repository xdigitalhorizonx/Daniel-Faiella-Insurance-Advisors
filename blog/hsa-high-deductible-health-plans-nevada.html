HSAs & high-deductible health plans: the triple tax advantage most Nevadans miss.

A higher deductible in exchange for a lower premium — and a savings account the IRS treats better than almost anything else. Here's how the two work together, the 2026 numbers, and who it actually fits.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · September 26, 2026 · 8 min read

When people compare health plans, they almost always look at one number: the monthly premium. It's the bill that shows up every month, so it feels like the cost of the plan. But there's a whole category of coverage built around a different trade — pay less every month in exchange for a higher deductible, and pair it with a savings account so powerful the IRS won't let you use it any other way. That's the high-deductible health plan and its partner, the Health Savings Account. Used well, it's one of the best deals in the tax code. Used carelessly, it can leave you exposed. Here's the honest version for Nevadans.

HDHP and HSA: the plan and the account

These two terms get blurred together, but they're different things doing different jobs. An HDHP is the insurance — a health plan with a higher deductible and, in exchange, a lower monthly premium. An HSA is a separate savings account you're only allowed to open and fund while you're enrolled in a qualifying HDHP. The plan is the coverage; the account is the money you set aside to meet the deductible and pay for care along the way. You need the plan to unlock the account — but once the account is open, the balance is yours for life, even if you later switch plans or leave the state.

This matters most for people buying their own coverage — the self-employed, contractors, and early retirees I write about in under-65 health insurance in Nevada. On the Nevada Health Link marketplace, HSA-eligible plans are usually flagged, and choosing one is what makes the whole strategy possible.

The triple tax advantage, plainly

No other account gets taxed this gently. The HSA is the only vehicle in the tax code that's tax-advantaged on all three sides:

  • Money goes in pre-tax. Contributions are deductible (or made straight from a paycheck before tax), lowering your taxable income the year you contribute.
  • Money grows tax-free. Interest and, once your balance clears the cash threshold, investment gains compound without being taxed year to year.
  • Money comes out tax-free — as long as it's spent on qualified medical expenses. Deductibles, copays, prescriptions, dental, vision, and more all count.

A 401(k) taxes you on the way out. A Roth taxes you on the way in. An HSA, used for healthcare, does neither. That's why so many planners treat a well-funded HSA as a stealth retirement account for medical costs — a bill everyone faces later in life.

Why Nevada makes the HSA even cleaner

Here's a wrinkle most people never hear: a few states, California and New Jersey among them, don't recognize HSAs and tax the growth at the state level. Nevada has no state income tax at all, so there's simply nothing for the state to touch — not your contributions, not your growth, not your withdrawals. Your HSA delivers its full federal triple tax advantage with zero state-level complications layered on top. For a self-employed Nevadan already enjoying no state tax on income, an HSA stacks one of the best federal tax breaks available onto an already tax-friendly home base. It's the same reason the state's tax climate quietly improves the math on tax-deferred retirement tools like annuities, too.

The 2026 numbers

The IRS resets these figures most years. For 2026, here's what qualifies and how much you can put away:

2026 limitSelf-onlyFamily
Max HSA contribution$4,400$8,750
Catch-up (age 55+)+$1,000+$1,000
Minimum HDHP deductible$1,700$3,400
HDHP out-of-pocket max$8,500$17,000

A plan only counts as an HDHP — and only makes you HSA-eligible — if it meets both the minimum-deductible and out-of-pocket rules above. That's why you can't just call any plan with a big deductible an "HSA plan." Confirm the plan is HSA-qualified before you count on the account.

Who it fits — and who it doesn't

An HDHP-plus-HSA isn't automatically the smart pick. It's a fit when a few things line up, and a poor fit when they don't.

It tends to fit people who are relatively healthy and don't use a lot of care, who can comfortably cover the deductible out of pocket if a bad year hits, and who have the discipline to actually bank the premium savings into the HSA instead of spending them. For a healthy self-employed Nevadan, the lower premium plus the tax break can be a genuine winner.

It fits less well for someone who expects heavy medical use, takes expensive ongoing medications, is planning a surgery or a baby, or simply couldn't absorb the full deductible without financial strain. In those cases the lower premium is a mirage — you'll likely spend the difference and then some at the point of care. A lower-deductible plan, even at a higher premium, can be the cheaper choice overall.

One more honest caution: a high deductible means real money is on the line before the plan pays in full. That's exactly the gap that critical illness and accident insurance is designed to backfill — a lump-sum cash benefit that can cover the deductible if a serious diagnosis or injury lands. Pairing a lean HDHP with a small supplemental policy is a combination worth weighing.

The HSA doesn't disappear — it follows you

Unlike a flexible spending account, an HSA has no "use it or lose it" rule. Whatever you don't spend rolls over year after year, keeps growing, and stays yours even if you change jobs, change plans, or move. Many people intentionally pay small medical bills out of pocket in their working years and let the HSA compound for later. The one hard stop to plan around is Medicare: once you enroll in any part of Medicare, you can no longer contribute to an HSA — though you can still spend the balance tax-free, including on many Medicare premiums. Because Part A can be backdated, this is a real timing trap for people who work past 65, which I break down in signing up for Medicare while still working.

How I'd walk you through it

The decision comes down to two honest questions, and no plan talk until we've answered them.

Question one: how much care do you realistically expect to use? Your last couple of years of medical spending are the best predictor. If it's low and steady, an HDHP's premium savings likely outrun the higher deductible. If it's high or about to be, a richer plan usually wins.

Question two: could you cover the deductible tomorrow? If a surprise bill up to the out-of-pocket max wouldn't derail you — and you'll bank the premium savings — the HSA strategy can be excellent. If it would, the lower premium isn't worth the exposure.

Good questions

HSA and high-deductible plan questions, answered straight.

What is the difference between an HDHP and an HSA?

An HDHP is a type of health insurance plan with a higher deductible and lower premium — it is the coverage. An HSA is a separate tax-advantaged savings account you can only open and contribute to while you are enrolled in a qualifying HDHP — it is the money. The plan protects you against a catastrophic year; the account gives you a tax-favored way to pay the deductible and everyday costs in the meantime. You need the HDHP to be eligible for the HSA, but the HSA itself is yours to keep for life.

How much can I contribute to an HSA in 2026?

For 2026, the IRS allows HSA contributions of up to $4,400 for self-only HDHP coverage and up to $8,750 for family coverage. If you are 55 or older, you can add an extra $1,000 catch-up contribution. To qualify, your health plan must meet the 2026 HDHP rules: a minimum deductible of $1,700 for self-only or $3,400 for family coverage, with out-of-pocket maximums no higher than $8,500 self-only or $17,000 family. These figures are set by the IRS and change most years.

Does Nevada tax my HSA?

No. Nevada has no state income tax, so there is nothing for the state to tax on your HSA contributions, growth, or withdrawals. That is actually an advantage over a handful of states, such as California and New Jersey, that do tax HSA earnings. In Nevada your HSA delivers its full federal triple tax advantage — pre-tax contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses — with no state-level complications on top.

Is a high-deductible health plan a good idea?

It depends on your health, your cash flow, and your discipline. An HDHP paired with an HSA tends to fit people who are relatively healthy, can comfortably cover the deductible if a bad year hits, and want to bank the premium savings in a tax-advantaged account. It fits less well for someone who expects heavy medical use, takes costly ongoing medications, or would struggle to pay the deductible out of pocket. The lower premium is only a real savings if an unexpected bill would not derail your finances.

What happens to my HSA when I go on Medicare?

Once you enroll in any part of Medicare you can no longer contribute to an HSA, though you can keep the account and spend it tax-free on qualified expenses, including many Medicare premiums. Because Part A enrollment can be backdated up to six months, people who work past 65 and want to keep contributing need to plan the timing carefully — a trap covered in our article on signing up for Medicare while still working. The balance you have already built is always yours to keep and use.
