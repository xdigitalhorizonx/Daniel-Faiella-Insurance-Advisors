Long-Term Care

What long-term care really costs in Nevada.

It's the retirement expense almost no one plans for — and in Nevada, it runs higher than the national average. Here are the real numbers for 2026, and the four ways Northern Nevada families actually pay for care.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · October 12, 2026 · 7 min read

Most people spend decades planning for the fun parts of retirement — the travel, the grandkids, the garden — and almost no time planning for the part that's statistically most likely to drain the savings behind all of it: long-term care. About seven in ten people turning 65 will need some form of long-term care in their lives, and in Nevada the price tag for that care has climbed faster than almost anyone expects. If you've spent a career building a nest egg in Northern Nevada, understanding these numbers is the first step to protecting it.

So let's start with the part nobody puts on a brochure: what care actually costs here, in real dollars.

The numbers most Nevada families underestimate

These are the most recent median figures from the Genworth and CareScout Cost of Care Survey (2024 data, the latest published), for the state of Nevada. Two things stand out: care is expensive, and Nevada runs above the national median in almost every category.

Read that nursing-home line again. A private room in Nevada runs more than $150,000 a year — and these are medians, meaning half of facilities cost more. Even "staying home," which most people assume is the cheap option, isn't: a full-time home health aide in Nevada now runs close to $87,000 a year, because the cost is in the caregiver's hours, not the real estate.

Why Nevada runs higher than average

Nevada's costs didn't just edge up — several categories jumped sharply year over year, with home health aide costs rising about 27% and assisted living about 22% in a single survey cycle. A few forces push the state above the national median: a tight caregiver labor market across the Reno–Carson City corridor, strong demand from one of the fastest-growing retiree populations in the country, and the simple fact that rural parts of Northern Nevada have fewer facilities competing on price. For families in Carson City, Reno, and the Carson Valley, that means budgeting for care at the higher end of the national range, not the middle.

The myth that Medicare covers it

Here's the most expensive misunderstanding in retirement planning: the belief that Medicare will pay for long-term care. It won't — not the kind most people end up needing. Medicare covers short-term skilled care, up to 100 days in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying hospital stay, with cost-sharing kicking in after day 20. What it does not cover is custodial care: the ongoing, non-medical help with bathing, dressing, eating, and getting around that defines most long-term care. I walk through exactly where that line falls in does Medicare cover long-term care? — but the short version is that the longest, costliest care is the care Medicare leaves entirely to you.

The four ways families actually pay for care

Once you accept that the bill is real and Medicare won't cover it, the question becomes simple: where does the money come from? In practice, there are four sources, and most families end up using some combination.

  • Out of pocket. Savings, investments, and retirement income. This is the default — and the one that quietly erodes an estate. A couple of years of nursing-home care can consume a six-figure nest egg that took decades to build.
  • Long-term care insurance. A traditional LTC policy or a hybrid life/LTC policy pays a monthly or lump-sum benefit toward care, shifting the risk to an insurer. The hybrid versions solve the old "use it or lose it" complaint — if you never need care, the policy pays a death benefit instead.
  • Life insurance and annuity income. Many modern policies include living benefits or a chronic-illness rider that lets you accelerate part of the death benefit to pay for care, and a guaranteed-income annuity can provide a predictable stream that helps cover ongoing costs.
  • Medicaid. The government safety net — but only after you've spent down most of your assets to qualify. More on that below.

Medicaid is a safety net, not a plan

Nevada Medicaid does pay for nursing-facility care and some home- and community-based services — but it's need-based, which means you generally have to spend down to roughly $2,000 in countable assets (with protections for a spouse who stays in the home) before you qualify. Medicaid also applies a five-year look-back on assets you gave away or sold below value, which can create a penalty period. In other words, Medicaid works — but by the time it kicks in, much of what you were trying to leave your family may already be gone. It's the floor, not the plan, and the exact limits change year to year, so always confirm current figures before counting on them.

The best time to plan is before you need it

The cruel math of long-term care is that the moment you need it is the worst moment to arrange it. Coverage gets expensive or unavailable once health declines, and decisions made in the middle of a crisis rarely protect a family the way a plan made over coffee does. The practical window to put coverage in place is usually your mid-50s to mid-60s, while premiums are reasonable and your health still qualifies you. Even if you intend to self-fund, mapping out exactly how you'd cover a care event — and which of your assets you'd draw on first — turns a frightening unknown into a manageable line item. Good questions

Nevada long-term care costs, answered straight.

How much does long-term care cost in Nevada?

Nevada's long-term care costs run above the national median. Based on the most recent Genworth and CareScout Cost of Care Survey (2024 data), the annual median in Nevada was about $73,320 for assisted living, roughly $86,944 for a home health aide, about $134,503 for a semi-private nursing home room, and about $153,483 for a private nursing home room. That works out to roughly $6,100 a month for assisted living and nearly $12,800 a month for a private nursing home room, and costs have generally continued to rise since.

Does Medicare pay for long-term care in Nevada?

Mostly no. Medicare covers short-term skilled care — up to 100 days in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying hospital stay, with cost-sharing after day 20 — but it does not pay for ongoing custodial long-term care, which is help with everyday activities like bathing, dressing, and eating. Since custodial care is what most people eventually need, Medicare leaves the largest and longest costs uncovered, and families cover them through savings, long-term care insurance, or Medicaid.

Does Nevada Medicaid cover nursing home care?

Yes, Nevada Medicaid pays for nursing facility care and some home- and community-based care for people who meet strict income and asset limits — typically around $2,000 in countable assets for an individual, with protections for a spouse who remains at home. Medicaid also uses a five-year look-back period that can penalize assets given away or sold below value before applying. Because it is need-based, Medicaid is best understood as a safety net rather than a plan, and the exact limits change, so confirm current figures before relying on them.

How do most families pay for long-term care?

In practice, families pay for care four main ways: out of pocket from savings and retirement income; with long-term care insurance (traditional or a hybrid life/LTC policy); by accelerating a life insurance benefit or using annuity income; or through Medicaid once assets are spent down. Most people end up using a combination, and planning ahead is what keeps the choice from defaulting to spending down savings and relying on Medicaid.

When should I start planning for long-term care?

The practical window to buy long-term care coverage is usually your mid-50s to mid-60s, while premiums are more affordable and before health conditions can make coverage expensive or unavailable. But planning isn't only about insurance — even if you self-fund, mapping out how you'd pay for a care event, and documenting your wishes, is worth doing earlier. The worst time to start is in the middle of a crisis, when options narrow and decisions get made under pressure.
