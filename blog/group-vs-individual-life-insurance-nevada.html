Life Insurance

Your work life insurance isn't yours. That's the problem.

The policy through your job is cheap, easy, and easy to forget about — until you change employers and it quietly disappears. Here's how group and individual life insurance really compare for Nevada families, and why most people need both.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · October 26, 2026 · 6 min read

For a lot of working Nevadans, the only life insurance they have is the policy that came with the job. You checked a box during open enrollment, maybe bumped up the amount a little, and filed it under "handled." It's an easy thing to believe, because group life insurance really is a genuine benefit — often free, and requiring nothing more than a signature.

But "handled" and "enough" are two very different things. The coverage through your employer is a fine starting point and a poor finish line, and the reason comes down to one word that almost nobody notices on the enrollment form: the policy isn't yours. It belongs to your employer's plan. Understanding what that means — before you ever need the coverage — is the difference between a family that's protected and one that only thought it was.

1. What group life insurance actually is

Group life insurance is a single master policy your employer buys to cover everyone on the team. It usually comes in two layers. The first is basic coverage the company pays for — commonly a flat amount or one to two times your annual salary. The second is supplemental (or voluntary) coverage you can buy through payroll, often in multiples of your salary, sometimes with coverage for a spouse and children.

Its best feature is the easy on-ramp. Up to a "guaranteed issue" limit, you can usually get the basic and a chunk of supplemental coverage with no medical exam and no health questions. For someone with a health condition that makes an individual policy expensive or hard to get, that guaranteed-issue door is genuinely valuable. The trouble starts with everything the enrollment form doesn't spell out.

2. The four catches nobody reads

Group life insurance has four limitations that matter enormously the day your family actually needs the money — and all four are invisible when you're healthy and employed.

  • It's tied to the job, not to you. When you change employers, get laid off, or retire, the coverage usually ends or drops to a token amount. People change jobs far more often than they change their need for life insurance.
  • The amount is usually too small. One or two times salary sounds like a lot until you measure it against a mortgage, years of replacing a paycheck, and the cost of raising children. It often covers a fraction of what a family truly needs.
  • The price isn't locked. Supplemental group coverage is typically priced in age bands, so your cost climbs every few years — the opposite of an individual term policy, where the premium is fixed for the whole term.
  • It's thin on features. Group coverage rarely carries the riders that make modern life insurance flexible, and you can't tailor it, name it into a trust the way you'd like, or count on it staying put.

There's also a quiet tax wrinkle: under IRS rules, employer-paid coverage above $50,000 creates a small amount of "imputed income" added to your W-2. It's usually modest — and because Nevada has no state income tax, there's no state layer stacked on top — but it's a reminder that even "free" coverage comes with fine print.

3. Group vs. individual, side by side

Neither type is "better" in the abstract — they're built for different jobs. Here's how they stack up on the things that decide whether coverage is actually there when it's needed.

4. The moment it all comes due: changing jobs

The weakness of group coverage shows up at the worst possible time — a layoff, a career move, or retirement. On your way out, most plans offer one of two escape hatches, and both run on a tight clock, usually about 30 to 31 days from your last day:

  • Portability lets you keep the group coverage as term insurance, but now you pay the full cost yourself, often at a steep rate.
  • Conversion lets you turn the group coverage into an individual permanent policy with no new medical exam — the same guaranteed-health idea behind term life conversion, which is a lifeline if your health has changed.

Both are better than nothing, but they're reactive, expensive, and easy to miss in the chaos of a job change. An individual policy you already own skips the whole scramble. That's also why life insurance decisions heading into retirement go so much smoother when your coverage was never hitched to an employer in the first place.

5. The right answer is usually "both"

This isn't an argument against group coverage — it's an argument for knowing its job. Think of it as a sensible two-layer plan:

Group coverage is the bonus layer. Take the free basic amount, and use supplemental group coverage when your health makes individual underwriting difficult or when you need a quick, temporary bump. It's a great deal for what it is.

An individual policy is the foundation. It's sized to your real obligations, locked in at today's age and health, and it belongs to you — so a job change, a layoff, or a future diagnosis can't take it away. For most families a level term policy does the heavy lifting, and many individual policies can carry living-benefit riders that let you tap the death benefit early if a serious illness strikes — something group coverage almost never offers. If you own a business, the same logic extends to buy-sell and key-person coverage that protects the company, not just the family.

6. Why this matters a little more in Nevada

Northern Nevada's economy runs on movement. Gaming and hospitality, seasonal and construction work, the warehouse and logistics boom around Reno and Sparks, and a steady flow of families relocating to Carson City and the Carson Valley — all of it means people here change employers often. Every one of those transitions is a moment when job-based coverage can lapse and an owned policy keeps right on protecting the family. Nevada's lack of a state income tax is a small bonus on top, keeping both the imputed-income wrinkle on large group policies and the tax picture around your overall plan simpler than it would be in a higher-tax state.

Good questions

Work life insurance questions, answered straight.

Is the life insurance through my job enough?

For most families, no — not on its own. Employer-paid group life is often just one to two times your salary, which rarely covers a mortgage plus years of lost income and raising children. It's a valuable free benefit, but it works best as a supplement to an individual policy you own, not as your entire plan. The honest test is to compare your total group coverage against what your family would actually need if your paycheck disappeared tomorrow.

What happens to my group life insurance when I leave my job?

In most cases it ends, or shrinks sharply, once you leave or retire. Many group plans offer a limited right to "port" the coverage (continue it as group term at your own cost) or to "convert" it to an individual permanent policy without a new medical exam, but you usually have only 30 to 31 days from your last day to act, and ported or converted rates are often much higher. Because those windows are short and easy to miss, it's safer to carry your own individual policy that moves with you no matter where you work.

Do I pay taxes on my employer's group life insurance?

The death benefit your beneficiaries receive is generally income-tax-free. But under IRS rules, employer-paid group term life coverage above $50,000 creates "imputed income" — a small taxable amount added to your W-2 based on an IRS age-based rate table, not on what the coverage is actually worth. It's usually modest, but it's one reason very large amounts of employer-paid coverage aren't quite as free as they look. Nevada has no state income tax, so there's no state layer on top of it.

Should I buy supplemental life insurance through work or an individual policy?

It depends on your health and your plans. Supplemental group coverage is convenient and often requires little or no underwriting, which helps if your health makes individual coverage hard to get. But supplemental group rates usually rise as you age and still disappear when you leave the employer. If you're in reasonably good health, an individually underwritten policy often locks in a level premium for decades and belongs to you — so it's frequently the better foundation, with supplemental group coverage layered on top.
