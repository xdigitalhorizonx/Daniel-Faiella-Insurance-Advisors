Estate Planning

In a blended family, "everything to my spouse" can quietly disinherit your kids.

Second marriages, stepchildren, and Nevada's community-property rules make the simple plan the risky one. Here's how to provide for the spouse you have now and the children you had before — without asking anyone to trust that it'll all work out.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor & Estate Advisor, Carson City NV · September 30, 2026 · 8 min read

Here's a plan I see all the time in second marriages: "When I go, everything goes to my spouse. When they go, it gets split between all the kids." It sounds fair. It's simple. Everyone at the table nods. And in a blended family, it's one of the most reliable ways to accidentally disinherit your own children.

I'm Daniel Faiella, an independent insurance broker and estate advisor in Carson City. I don't draft wills or trusts — a licensed Nevada estate attorney does that — but helping families coordinate the pieces of a legacy plan is a big part of my work, and blended families are where the pieces most often don't fit. The good news: this is a very solvable problem, and life insurance is usually the simplest part of the solution.

How the accidental disinheritance happens

Picture a Minden couple — call them Mark and Linda. Each has two grown children from a first marriage. Their wills leave everything to each other, and then to "all four kids equally." Mark passes first, and everything he owned goes to Linda, just as planned.

Now it's Linda's money, outright. Nothing legally requires her to keep the old plan. Over the next fifteen years she might update her will, move closer to her own kids, add one of them to her accounts, or remarry. But when Linda passes, Mark's children inherit only what Linda's final documents say, which may be nothing.

And if there's no will at all, it's worse. Under Nevada's intestacy rules, stepchildren generally don't inherit from a stepparent unless they were legally adopted. Whatever passes to the surviving spouse tends to end up with that spouse's bloodline.

The Nevada wrinkle: community property

Nevada is one of nine community property states, and that shapes blended-family planning in three practical ways:

  • You only control your half of community property. Most assets earned or bought during the marriage belong to both spouses equally, no matter whose name is on the account. You can leave your half to whomever you choose, but not your spouse's half.
  • Separate property can quietly stop being separate. A house you owned before the marriage, or an inheritance you received, is generally your separate property — until it gets mixed with marital money. Refinance it jointly, pay the mortgage from a joint account, or deposit an inheritance into a shared checking account, and that clean line gets blurry fast.
  • Life insurance can be partly your spouse's. If premiums on your policy are paid with community funds, your spouse may have a community-property claim to part of the death benefit — even if you named your children as beneficiaries. The fix is simple but often skipped: get your spouse's written consent to the beneficiary designation, and keep it with the policy.

Retirement plans add another layer. For a 401(k) or other employer plan governed by federal ERISA law, your spouse is automatically the beneficiary unless they sign a notarized waiver. Naming your kids on the form alone isn't enough.

Why life insurance is the cleanest equalizer

The core tension in a blended family is that the same pool of assets usually has to do two jobs: support the surviving spouse for what could be decades, and eventually reach the children from each side.

Life insurance solves that by creating a second pot. A common structure looks like this: the home and retirement accounts go to (or in trust for) the surviving spouse, and a life insurance policy names the children from the first marriage directly. Everyone is provided for at the same moment, and nobody's inheritance depends on someone else's goodwill years later.

It works especially well because the death benefit:

  • Passes by contract, outside probate. It goes straight to the named beneficiary, usually within weeks, and isn't part of a public court file.
  • Is generally income-tax-free to the beneficiary under federal law — and Nevada has no state income, estate, or inheritance tax on top of that.
  • Is predictable. You know the number in advance, so you can size it to equal what the other side of the family will eventually receive.

How big should the policy be, and what kind? If you're in your 40s or 50s and the main goal is covering the years until the surviving spouse's own assets stretch far enough, term coverage can be very affordable. If the goal is a guaranteed inheritance no matter when you pass, permanent coverage is usually the better tool. I walk through that trade-off in term vs. whole life vs. IUL, and through the sizing math in how much life insurance you actually need. If you're already retired, life insurance in retirement covers what's still available and affordable.

Matching each asset to the right job

In practice, most blended-family plans come down to deciding which asset goes to which person, and how:

Trusts that protect both sides

For the house and larger investment accounts, the attorney-drafted tool that does the heavy lifting is usually a trust. Instead of leaving assets to your spouse outright, you can leave them in a trust that gives your spouse income, and even the right to live in the home, for the rest of their life — with whatever remains going to your children afterward. Your spouse is fully cared for, and the ultimate destination is locked in. If you haven't set up a trust yet, start with do you need a living trust in Nevada?

Annuities can play a quieter supporting role. A surviving spouse who has guaranteed lifetime income is under much less pressure to spend down the assets meant for the kids. If that's part of your plan, fixed vs. indexed annuities is a good primer.

For very large estates, an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) can hold a policy outside your taxable estate. With the federal estate-tax exemption at $15 million per person in 2026 and Nevada having no estate tax, most families won't need one for tax reasons.

Five mistakes I see in second-marriage plans

  • An ex-spouse still on a beneficiary form. Nevada law revokes many designations to a former spouse automatically at divorce, but not for 401(k)s and other ERISA plans, where the form on file wins. Check every form yourself. I cover this in detail in how a beneficiary form can override your will.
  • Naming a minor child directly. A Nevada court would need to appoint a guardian of the estate before the child can receive anything. Use a trust or a custodian under Nevada's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act instead.
  • "My spouse will take care of my kids." Maybe. But a plan that depends on a promise isn't a plan. Put it in writing.
  • Treating the kids "equally" when their needs aren't equal. Children from a first marriage may be adults with their own homes, while a younger child from the current marriage may still need college money. Fair and identical aren't always the same.
  • Never having the family conversation. Surprises at a funeral are where blended-family lawsuits start.

Where to start

Sit down with your spouse and write out three lists: what each of you brought into the marriage, what you've built together, and who you each want to provide for. Then pull every beneficiary form: life insurance, annuities, IRAs, 401(k)s. That exercise alone usually shows exactly where the gaps are. From there, a Nevada estate attorney drafts the documents, and I help size and place the insurance so the math actually works for both sides of the family. Good questions

Blended-family estate planning, answered straight.

Do stepchildren inherit in Nevada if there's no will?

Generally, no. Under Nevada's intestacy rules, stepchildren don't inherit from a stepparent unless they were legally adopted. If you want stepchildren to receive part of your estate, you need to name them in a will, a trust, or on a beneficiary designation.

Can I leave my life insurance to my children from a prior marriage instead of my spouse?

Yes. You can name anyone as a life insurance beneficiary. In Nevada, though, if premiums were paid with community funds, your spouse may have a community-property claim to part of the proceeds. Getting your spouse's written consent to the beneficiary designation, and keeping it with the policy, helps prevent that dispute.

What's the best way to provide for both my spouse and my children?

A common approach uses two tools. A trust gives your spouse income or the right to live in the home for life, with the remainder going to your children. A separate life insurance policy names your children (or a trust for them) directly, so they receive an inheritance right away instead of waiting for the surviving spouse to pass.

Does divorce remove my ex-spouse as a beneficiary in Nevada?

Not reliably. Nevada law revokes many revocable designations to a former spouse after divorce, but federal ERISA law governs 401(k)s and most employer plans, and there the beneficiary form on file controls. The safe move is to update every beneficiary form yourself after a divorce or remarriage.
