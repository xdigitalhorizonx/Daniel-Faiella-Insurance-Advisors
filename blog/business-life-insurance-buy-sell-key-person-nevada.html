Life Insurance

Who buys your half of the business the day after you're gone?

A partner's death or the loss of a key employee can unravel a healthy Nevada business overnight. Here's how buy-sell agreements and key-person life insurance keep the company — and your family's stake in it — intact.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · September 20, 2026 · 8 min read

Two partners built a contracting business in Reno over fifteen years — steady crews, a good name, a shop off Mill Street. Then one of them died suddenly. The surviving partner grieved a friend, and then discovered he now co-owned the company with his friend's widow, who had never worked a day in the business and, understandably, wanted her husband's share turned into cash. There was no agreement on what that share was worth, and no money set aside to buy it. Within a year, a profitable company was sold under pressure for far less than it was worth. Everyone lost.

That story plays out somewhere every year, and it's almost always preventable. If you own a business with partners, or if your company would stumble badly without one particular person, the most important life insurance in your life may not be the policy that protects your family at home — it's the one that protects the business those people depend on. Here's how two tools, a buy-sell agreement and key-person coverage, do that work.

The asset nobody insures: the business itself

Most owners insure the building, the trucks, the inventory, and their liability. Then they leave the single largest asset — the ownership stake itself, often the biggest thing on the family's balance sheet — completely exposed to the one event that's certain to happen eventually. When an owner dies, the share doesn't disappear. It passes to their estate and then to their heirs, and now the surviving owners are in business with people who may have no interest in the work and every interest in being paid out. Meanwhile the bank gets nervous, key customers wonder who's in charge, and the best employees quietly update their résumés. The company can be perfectly healthy and still come apart, purely because there was no plan and no cash to execute one.

Buy-sell agreements, funded with life insurance

A buy-sell agreement is a contract among the owners that answers two questions in advance: what happens to an owner's share when they die, become disabled, or leave, and what that share is worth. Funding it with life insurance is what turns a good document into a working plan. Each owner is insured for the value of their stake; when one dies, the tax-free death benefit provides the exact cash needed to buy that share from the family at the price everyone already agreed to. The family gets fair value in cash instead of an illiquid stake they can't use; the surviving owners keep full control of the company they've built. There are two common ways to structure it:

  • Cross-purchase. Each owner personally owns a policy on each of the other owners and buys the departing owner's share directly. This can give the surviving owners a stepped-up cost basis, which matters if they ever sell — but it gets unwieldy fast once you have more than two or three owners, since the number of policies multiplies.
  • Entity-purchase (stock redemption). The business itself owns the policies and buys back the departing owner's share. It's much simpler to administer when there are several owners, since the company holds one policy per owner rather than everyone insuring everyone.

Which one is right depends on how many owners you have, your entity type, and your tax goals — decisions that belong with your attorney and CPA. My role is to make sure the coverage funding it is sized correctly and actually in force, so the agreement isn't just a nice paragraph in a binder no one funded.

Key-person coverage: protecting the irreplaceable

Not every critical person is an owner. Many Nevada businesses have a key person — a top salesperson who carries half the client relationships, a master technician the whole shop is built around, a general manager who is effectively the operation. If that person died tomorrow, revenue would drop, projects would stall, and the cost of recruiting and training a replacement would land all at once. Key-person insurance is a policy the business owns and pays for, on that individual, with the business as the beneficiary. The death benefit gives the company breathing room: cash to cover the revenue dip, to recruit and train a successor, to reassure a lender whose loan may hinge on that person, and to keep customers and staff steady through the transition. Lenders and investors increasingly ask for it before they'll extend credit — for a lot of small businesses, the founder is the collateral.

Why this hits differently in Nevada

Nevada is a business owner's state, and that's exactly why this planning matters here. There's no state personal or corporate income tax, which is part of what keeps drawing companies to Reno, Sparks and the Tahoe-Reno Industrial Center — and it means there's no state income tax layered on top of the generally income-tax-free federal treatment of a life insurance death benefit. Our economy leans heavily on small and closely held businesses: contractors, restaurants and breweries, ranching and ag operations in the Carson Valley, professional practices and family shops across Carson City. Many are partnerships or two-owner operations — precisely the setup where an unfunded buy-sell does the most damage. One more Nevada wrinkle: for married owners, our community property rules can affect who actually inherits a business interest and how a policy should be owned, which is another reason to get the structure right up front rather than discover it during a crisis.

What it costs, and how to set it up right

The reassuring part is that a lot of this is funded with term life insurance, which is inexpensive relative to the risk it covers — a healthy owner in their 40s can often insure a substantial share for a modest monthly premium. Some situations call for permanent coverage instead, particularly when the need is lifelong or the policy's cash value is meant to help fund a buyout at retirement rather than only at death. A few points that make or break these plans:

  • Value the business first. The coverage should track a realistic, current valuation — and both should be revisited as the company grows, so a policy written five years ago doesn't cover a fraction of today's worth.
  • Coordinate the document and the funding. An attorney drafts the buy-sell; a CPA weighs the tax structure; I make sure the policies are the right size, type, and ownership to actually deliver the cash the agreement promises. All three have to line up.
  • Mind the federal rules. Employer-owned life insurance must meet the notice-and-consent requirements of IRC Section 101(j) for the death benefit to stay income-tax-free. It's a simple form done before the policy is issued — and a costly thing to miss afterward.
  • Review it like you review your books. Owners change, values change, key people come and go. A plan checked every couple of years stays a plan; one left in a drawer slowly becomes fiction.

Where it fits in the bigger picture

Business coverage doesn't replace the personal protection your family needs — it sits alongside it. The same review that sizes your buy-sell should look at your personal life insurance, your income protection if a disability rather than a death takes you out of the business, your eventual retirement income as you plan an exit, and your beneficiary designations and estate plan so the business and the family fit together instead of colliding. That's the kind of coordinated review I do with owners across Northern Nevada — the full picture on my life insurance page — so the company you spent a career building outlives any one person in it, you included.

Good questions

Business life insurance, answered straight.

What is a buy-sell agreement, and why fund it with life insurance?

A buy-sell agreement is a legal contract that spells out what happens to an owner's share of a business if they die, become disabled, or leave. Funding it with life insurance means that when an owner dies, the policy pays a tax-free lump sum the surviving owners or the business use to buy that share from the deceased owner's family — at a price already agreed to. Without funding, the business has to find that cash on its own, which often means loans, a fire sale, or forcing the late owner's spouse into the company as an unwanted partner.

What is key-person life insurance?

Key-person insurance is a life insurance policy the business owns on an owner or an employee whose knowledge, relationships, or sales are hard to replace. The business pays the premiums and is the beneficiary. If that person dies, the death benefit gives the company cash to cover lost revenue, recruit and train a replacement, reassure lenders and customers, and stay solvent through the transition.

Cross-purchase or entity-purchase — which buy-sell structure is better?

In a cross-purchase, each owner buys a policy on each of the other owners and buys their share directly. In an entity-purchase (stock redemption), the business owns the policies and buys back the departing owner's share. Cross-purchase can offer a favorable cost basis for the survivors but gets complicated with more than two or three owners; entity-purchase is simpler to administer with several owners. The right structure depends on the number of owners, the entity type, and tax goals, so set it with your attorney and CPA.

Does Nevada's lack of a state income tax change how this is taxed?

It helps at the state level: Nevada has no state personal or corporate income tax, so there's no state income tax layered on top of the federal treatment. Life insurance death benefits are also generally received income-tax-free at the federal level. Federal rules still apply — key-person and employer-owned policies must meet the notice-and-consent requirements under IRC Section 101(j) for the benefit to stay tax-free — so coordinate the setup with a tax professional.
