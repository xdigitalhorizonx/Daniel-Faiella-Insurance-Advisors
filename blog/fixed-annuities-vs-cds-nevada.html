Annuities

Fixed annuity or CD? Where to keep your safe money.

Both protect your principal and pay a guaranteed rate — but a bank CD and a fixed annuity are taxed, guaranteed, and accessed in very different ways. Here's how Nevada savers decide which belongs with which dollar.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · October 10, 2026 · 7 min read

When you've worked hard to build savings, a chunk of it usually ends up labeled safe money — the part you refuse to put at risk in the stock market. The question is where that safe money should actually live. For most Nevada savers it comes down to two familiar choices: a bank CD or a fixed annuity. On the surface they look almost identical — lock in money, earn a guaranteed rate, get your principal back. Underneath, they behave differently enough that picking the wrong one can quietly cost you growth, flexibility, or thousands in avoidable tax.

This is a close cousin of the choice I cover in fixed vs. fixed indexed annuities — but here the comparison is simpler and more concrete: the insurance-company version of a CD, head to head with the bank's.

What each one actually is

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a bank product. You deposit a lump sum for a set term — anywhere from a few months to five years — and the bank pays a fixed interest rate. Your deposit is insured by the FDIC up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, which is why CDs are the gold standard of "can't lose it."

A fixed annuity — specifically a multi-year guaranteed annuity, or MYGA — is the insurance-company equivalent. You hand an insurer a lump sum, and it guarantees a fixed rate for a set term, commonly three, five, or seven years. Instead of FDIC insurance, your money is backed by the insurer's own reserves and financial strength, with the Nevada Life and Health Insurance Guaranty Association as a statutory backstop if a carrier ever fails. A MYGA is built to do one job well: grow a lump sum safely at a known rate.

The rate — and the tax advantage behind it

MYGAs frequently pay a bit more than comparable-term CDs, because insurers invest in longer-dated, higher-yielding bonds and pass some of that along. But the rate on the brochure is only half the story. The quieter advantage is tax deferral. A CD generates a 1099 every year, and you owe federal income tax on that interest whether or not you touch it. A MYGA's interest, by contrast, compounds untaxed until you take it out — so you're earning a return on money that would otherwise have gone to the IRS each year.

Here's where Nevada matters. Because our state has no state income tax, the state-tax piece is a wash either way — neither a CD nor an annuity is taxed by Nevada. But the federal deferral still does two valuable things: it lets your balance compound faster, and it lets you choose which year you realize the income. For a retiree, deciding when to trigger taxable income can help manage how much of your Social Security is taxed and keep you under the income thresholds that raise Medicare premiums — flexibility a CD simply doesn't give you.

Access and liquidity: the real trade-off

If there's one place a CD wins, it's simplicity of access. CDs are usually shorter, and breaking one early typically costs just a few months of interest. A fixed annuity asks for a longer commitment. Most MYGAs let you withdraw around 10% of the value each year without penalty, but pulling out more during the surrender-charge period triggers a declining surrender fee, and taking gains before age 59½ can add a 10% IRS penalty on top of ordinary income tax. None of that is a catch so much as a design feature: the annuity pays a stronger, tax-advantaged rate precisely because you're agreeing not to need the money for a while. The rule of thumb is simple — match the money to the time horizon. Dollars you might need next year belong in a CD or savings; dollars you won't touch for five-plus years can work harder in a MYGA.

Where each one fits a Nevada plan

Neither product is "better" — they're tools for different jobs, and plenty of savers use both:

  • Choose a CD for short time horizons, an emergency cushion beyond your checking account, or money you want kept simple and bank-based.
  • Choose a MYGA for a guaranteed, tax-deferred rate over several years on money you've mentally set aside for later — a natural fit for retirees and near-retirees protecting part of a nest egg.
  • Use both together by laddering: short CDs for near-term needs, longer MYGAs for the money that can wait, so something is always maturing while the rest keeps compounding.

A MYGA can also be a stepping stone. When the guarantee period ends, many savers roll the matured value into an annuity designed to produce guaranteed lifetime income, turning safe growth into a paycheck that can even cover an IRA's required withdrawals. It's one reason safe money, retirement income, and what you still need in retirement are best mapped out together rather than one product at a time.

Two things to check before you commit

Before you move safe money anywhere, two guardrails matter most:

  • Carrier strength and coverage limits. With a MYGA, you're relying on the insurer, so favor highly rated A-rated carriers and keep each contract within the guaranty association's protected limit. With a CD, stay within FDIC limits per bank.
  • The surrender schedule and renewal rate. Know exactly how long your annuity's surrender charges last, how much you can withdraw penalty-free, and what happens when the guarantee period ends — some contracts renew at a much lower rate, so you'll want a plan for that maturity date.

That comparison — current MYGA rates from strong carriers set next to today's best CDs, sized to your actual timeline — is exactly what I walk through with savers across Carson City, Reno, and Northern Nevada. Good questions

Fixed annuity vs. CD questions, answered straight.

Is a fixed annuity safer than a CD?

They're protected in different ways. A bank CD is backed by FDIC insurance up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank. A fixed or multi-year guaranteed annuity (MYGA) is backed by the issuing insurer's financial strength and, as a backstop, by the Nevada Life and Health Insurance Guaranty Association up to a statutory limit — currently $250,000 in present value for most annuity benefits per contract. Both are safe-money vehicles that protect your principal; the key is choosing a highly rated carrier and staying within the coverage limits.

Do fixed annuities pay more than CDs?

Often, yes. Multi-year guaranteed annuities frequently offer rates that edge out comparable-term bank CDs, and because the interest grows tax-deferred rather than being taxed every year, more of it compounds. But rates move with the broader interest-rate environment and vary by carrier and term, so the only honest answer is to compare current MYGA and CD quotes for the same term side by side.

How are fixed annuities and CDs taxed differently in Nevada?

CD interest is taxable at the federal level in the year it's earned, even if you leave it in the account. A fixed annuity's interest grows tax-deferred — you don't owe federal tax until you withdraw it. Nevada has no state income tax, so neither is taxed by the state, but the federal deferral still lets an annuity compound faster and lets you choose the year you take income, which can matter for Social Security and Medicare-related thresholds.

Can I get my money out of a fixed annuity early?

Yes, but with more friction than a CD. Most fixed annuities let you withdraw up to about 10% of the value each year penalty-free, but larger early withdrawals during the surrender-charge period incur a declining surrender fee. Withdrawing gains before age 59½ also generally triggers a 10% IRS penalty on top of ordinary income tax. CDs are typically shorter and simpler to exit, usually costing just a few months of interest — which is why annuities suit money you won't need for several years.

Who should consider a fixed annuity instead of a CD?

A fixed annuity tends to fit savers who want a guaranteed rate for several years, don't need immediate access to the money, and would benefit from tax-deferred growth — often retirees or near-retirees protecting a portion of their nest egg. A CD tends to fit shorter time horizons, money you may need soon, or funds you want kept simple and bank-based. Many Nevada savers use both, matching each dollar to when they'll need it.
