Long-Term Care

Long-term care insurance: the decision most families put off too long.

Medicare won't pay for an extended stay in a nursing home or years of home care. Here's how traditional LTC policies and hybrid life/LTC plans actually work, what drives the cost, and how Nevada protects your assets if you plan ahead.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · August 25, 2026 · 7 min read

I wrote recently about whether Medicare covers long-term care, and the honest answer is: barely, and not for long. Medicare's skilled-nursing benefit is capped at 100 days per benefit period and it never covers ongoing custodial help with bathing, dressing or meals — which is most of what long-term care actually is. So the question every family eventually faces isn't whether they'll need a plan for that risk. It's which plan, and whether they buy it while it's still affordable.

That's where long-term care insurance comes in, and it comes in two very different shapes: a traditional standalone policy or a hybrid life/LTC policy. Neither is universally better. Here's how to actually compare them.

1. Two ways to insure the same risk

Traditional long-term care insurance is a standalone contract, similar in spirit to auto or homeowners insurance. You pay an annual premium, and if you ever need qualifying care — typically defined as needing help with a set number of daily activities, or having a cognitive impairment — the policy reimburses covered costs up to your daily benefit and lifetime maximum. If you never need care, you don't get your premiums back. And unlike most insurance, the carrier can raise your premium in the future, on the entire class of policyholders, if claims run higher than priced — a real pattern in this industry over the past two decades.

Hybrid life/LTC policies (sometimes called asset-based or linked-benefit policies) attach a long-term care rider to a permanent life insurance policy. If you need care, you accelerate the death benefit to pay for it. If you never need care, your beneficiaries still receive a death benefit — the money isn't lost either way. Most hybrid designs also guarantee your premium will never increase, and some offer a return-of-premium option if you change your mind. The trade-off is cost: hybrids are usually funded with a single lump sum or a limited number of years of higher payments, rather than a low ongoing annual premium.

3. What actually drives the cost

Your age at purchase. This is the single biggest lever. Most carriers price coverage most favorably in your mid-50s to mid-60s. Every year you wait raises the premium — and raises the odds a new diagnosis reduces your options or gets you declined at underwriting entirely.

Benefit period and daily benefit. A 2-year benefit costs meaningfully less than a 5-year or lifetime benefit; a higher daily benefit amount costs more but matters more in a state where home care and facility costs keep climbing. National cost-of-care surveys have put private nursing home care well past $100,000 a year, with Nevada tracking close to the national median — a number worth pricing your benefit against, not against today's cost of a doctor's visit.

Elimination period. This works like a deductible measured in days, not dollars — typically 90 days of paying for care yourself before benefits begin. A longer elimination period lowers your premium but means budgeting for that gap out of savings.

Inflation protection. A policy purchased today with a flat benefit will buy noticeably less care in twenty years. A compound inflation rider costs more upfront but keeps your coverage realistic decades from now — arguably the single most important rider to not skip.

4. Nevada's Long-Term Care Partnership Program

Nevada participates in the National Long-Term Care Partnership Program, and it's one of the most underused planning tools I see. If you buy a Partnership-certified policy, every dollar it pays out in benefits becomes a dollar of your personal assets that Nevada Medicaid disregards later, if your coverage is ever exhausted and you need to apply. In plain terms: instead of spending down to Nevada's standard Medicaid asset limit before qualifying, you protect assets equal to what your policy already paid on your behalf. It's a meaningful bridge between "fully self-insured" and "fully reliant on Medicaid," and it only applies to policies that carry the Partnership certification — worth confirming before you buy.

5. Where people get it wrong

Waiting for "someday." The two biggest costs of waiting are a higher premium and the risk of being declined. I've had healthy 68-year-olds get turned down outright for a policy they could have easily qualified for at 58.

Buying flat, non-inflating coverage. A $200/day benefit sounds generous today. Priced against home care and facility costs two or three decades from now, without an inflation rider, it can fall well short right when you need it most.

Treating this as separate from the rest of the plan. Long-term care risk touches your estate plan, your retirement income strategy, and — if you're weighing a hybrid policy — the same permanent life insurance decisions I walk through in term vs. whole life vs. IUL. These aren't four separate conversations; they're one conversation with four moving parts.

Assuming self-insuring is free. Choosing to self-insure — covering care out of savings if the need arises — is a legitimate strategy for some households. It's rarely the free option people assume, though, once you price it against what a multi-year care need can actually cost in Nevada.

How I'd walk you through it

Three conversations, not a sales pitch.

Conversation one: the numbers. Your age, health, and what assets you're actually trying to protect — from a home to a retirement account you don't want spent down.

Conversation two: traditional vs. hybrid. Whether a lower ongoing premium or a guaranteed-value design fits your cash flow and your goals for the money if care never happens.

Conversation three: the fine print. Benefit period, elimination period, inflation protection, and whether Nevada Partnership certification applies — the details that decide whether the policy actually does what you bought it to do.

Good questions

Long-term care insurance questions, answered straight.

What's the difference between traditional LTC insurance and a hybrid life/LTC policy?

A traditional long-term care policy is a standalone contract: you pay a premium and, if you ever qualify for benefits, it reimburses covered care costs up to your daily and lifetime limits — but if you never need care, you generally get nothing back and premiums can be raised by the carrier over time. A hybrid life/LTC policy attaches a long-term care rider to a permanent life insurance policy: if you need care, you access the death benefit early to pay for it; if you never need care, your beneficiaries still receive a death benefit. Hybrid premiums are typically guaranteed not to increase.

How does Nevada's Long-Term Care Partnership Program work?

Nevada participates in the National Long-Term Care Partnership Program. If you buy a qualifying Partnership-certified policy, every dollar it pays out in benefits is a dollar of your personal assets that Nevada Medicaid disregards when it later evaluates your eligibility — so if your coverage is ever exhausted, you can qualify for Medicaid while protecting assets equal to what your policy already paid, instead of spending down to the standard Medicaid asset limit first.

When is the right time to buy long-term care insurance?

Most carriers price long-term care coverage most favorably in your mid-50s to mid-60s, while you're still healthy enough to qualify through underwriting. Waiting costs you twice: premiums climb with age, and a new health condition can reduce your options or get you declined outright. There's no single right age, but the window to buy affordably and get approved is narrower than most people assume.

Are long-term care insurance premiums tax-deductible?

Premiums for a federally tax-qualified long-term care policy may be deductible as a medical expense up to age-based limits set annually by the IRS, and benefits received are generally received income-tax-free. The rules differ for hybrid life/LTC policies and depend on your specific tax situation, so confirm current limits and your own eligibility with a tax advisor before assuming a deduction applies.
