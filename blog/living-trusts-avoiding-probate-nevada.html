A will doesn't skip probate in Nevada. A living trust does.

One of the most common things I hear at the kitchen table is, "We're fine — we have a will." A will is worth having. But it's not a skip-the-courthouse pass, and in Nevada the difference can cost your family the better part of a year.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor & Estate Advisor, Carson City NV · September 28, 2026 · 9 min read

Here's the misconception I spend more time gently correcting than almost any other: that a will keeps your family out of court. It doesn't. A will is the set of instructions a probate judge follows — it's the map for the process, not a way around it. If your home, your bank accounts, or your investment accounts are titled in your name alone when you pass, a will sends them into Nevada probate, not around it.

I'm Daniel Faiella, an independent insurance broker and estate advisor in Carson City. I don't draft trusts — a licensed Nevada estate attorney does that — but coordinating the pieces of a legacy plan is a core part of how I work with Northern Nevada families, and this is the conversation that changes the most minds. So let's walk through what probate actually is here, what a living trust changes, and why Nevada happens to be one of the best states in the country to hold one.

What probate actually is in Nevada

Probate is the court-supervised process of proving your will, paying your final debts, and transferring what's left to your heirs. In Nevada it runs through the district court, and even an uncomplicated case tends to take six months to a year — partly because the law requires a waiting period for creditors to come forward before anything can be distributed. A contested estate, or one with property in more than one state, can drag on well beyond that.

Three things about probate catch families off guard. First, it's public — the filings, the inventory of what you owned, and who inherited it all become part of the court record anyone can look up. Second, it's not free: there are filing and publication fees, and Nevada law ties the allowable attorney and personal-representative fees to the size of the estate, so bigger estates cost more to settle. Third, nothing gets distributed quickly — the people you wanted to help may wait most of a year for money that's sitting right there.

Nevada's probate tiers depend on the dollars

Not every estate lands in full-blown probate. Nevada scales the process to the size of the estate, and the thresholds matter:

Here's the catch for most Northern Nevada homeowners: with what a house in Carson City, Reno, or the Carson Valley is worth today, a single property can push you past the summary-administration line all by itself — before you've counted a vehicle, a savings account, or a brokerage. Plenty of people who think of themselves as "modest" estates are, on paper, full-probate estates.

What a revocable living trust changes

A revocable living trust is a legal container you create while you're alive and well. You move the title of your major assets — your home, bank and investment accounts — into the trust, and you keep complete control: you're the trustee, you can change or revoke it anytime, and for tax purposes nothing about your day-to-day life changes.

The difference shows up at two moments. If you become incapacitated, the successor trustee you named steps in and manages everything without a court-supervised guardianship. And when you pass, that same successor trustee distributes the assets directly to the people you named — privately, without probate, often in weeks rather than months. Because the trust owns the assets, there's nothing in your individual name for the probate court to administer.

That's the whole point: a living trust doesn't reduce your taxes or hide anything. It changes how your estate transfers — out of a public courtroom and into a private, faster hand-off.

Living trust vs. will — you usually want both

This isn't really a trust-or-will decision. Most solid Nevada plans use both, because they do different jobs:

  • The living trust holds your assets and keeps them out of probate, and manages them if you're incapacitated.
  • A "pour-over" will is the backstop — it catches any asset you forgot to retitle into the trust and sweeps it in. Just as importantly, a will is the only place you can name a guardian for minor children. A trust can't do that.
  • Powers of attorney and an advance directive round it out, naming who handles your finances and health decisions if you can't.

A trust with an empty gas tank goes nowhere, though — which brings us to the step people skip most.

Funding the trust is the step people miss

The single most common failure I see isn't not having a trust — it's having one that was never funded. Signing the trust document is only half the job. If you never actually retitle the house into the trust, or update the deed and account registrations, those assets are still in your individual name, and they'll go straight to probate no matter how nice the binder on your shelf looks. A trust only avoids probate for the assets you actually put inside it.

Why Nevada is a standout state for trusts

Nevada isn't just an ordinary place to hold a trust — it's regularly ranked among the best. A few reasons:

  • No state income tax, and no estate or inheritance tax. Beyond the federal estate-tax threshold, which affects a small share of families, what your heirs keep in Nevada tends to be more, not less, than in higher-tax states.
  • Strong asset-protection trusts. Nevada is one of the pioneers of the self-settled "asset protection" (spendthrift) trust, which — set up correctly and well in advance — can shield assets from future creditors in ways most states don't allow.
  • Dynasty trusts. Nevada lets a trust run for up to 365 years, so families can pass wealth across many generations without it dropping back into a taxable or probate-exposed estate each time.

These are attorney-drafted structures, not do-it-yourself projects — but they're exactly why Nevadans (and plenty of out-of-staters) choose to hold trusts here.

Where life insurance and annuities fit in

Here's the part I care about most as an advisor: some of your assets already skip probate without a trust. Life insurance and annuities pass by beneficiary designation, straight to the person you named, outside the court process entirely. That makes them some of the cleanest tools in a legacy plan — and it's why I treat them as core pieces of estate and legacy planning, not afterthoughts.

A trust and your beneficiary forms have to agree, though. Sometimes it makes sense to name your living trust as the beneficiary of a policy — for example, to control how a payout reaches young children or a beneficiary who isn't good with money. Other times naming a person directly is simpler and better. The wrong beneficiary form can quietly undo an expensive trust, which is exactly the mistake I walk through in how a beneficiary form can override your will. And if a chunk of your estate sits in an IRA, the way an annuity can smooth out required minimum distributions is worth understanding too — I cover that in how a guaranteed-income annuity can pay your IRA's RMDs.

So — do you need one?

If your estate is small enough to fit Nevada's affidavit or set-aside shortcuts and your assets already pass by beneficiary, you may be fine without a trust. But if you own a Nevada home, want to spare your family a public year in court, care about managing your affairs if you're incapacitated, or want the asset-protection and multi-generational tools Nevada is known for, a revocable living trust is usually worth a serious look. The right move is to sit down, map out what you own and how each piece would actually transfer, and bring in a Nevada estate attorney to draft the documents. That's the conversation I'm always glad to start.

Good questions

Living trusts & Nevada probate, answered straight.

Does a will avoid probate in Nevada?

No. A will doesn't avoid probate — it's the instruction manual the probate court follows. Assets that pass under a will still go through Nevada's probate process before they reach your heirs. The main tool for avoiding probate on assets like a home or bank account is a properly funded revocable living trust, which lets a successor trustee distribute those assets without court involvement.

How long does probate take in Nevada, and what does it cost?

Even a straightforward Nevada probate usually runs about six months to a year, in part because of a mandatory creditor-claim period, and larger or contested estates take longer. Costs include court filing fees, publication, and attorney and personal-representative fees that Nevada law ties to the size of the estate. Probate is also a public court record. A living trust is designed to skip all of that.

Do I still need a will if I have a living trust?

Yes. Even with a living trust you generally want a short "pour-over" will that catches anything you never retitled into the trust and, importantly, names guardians for minor children — something a trust cannot do. The two documents work together: the trust handles distribution and probate avoidance, the will provides a backstop and guardianship nominations.

Is a living trust worth it for a smaller Nevada estate?

It depends on what you own. Nevada offers simplified procedures for smaller estates — a small-estate affidavit for estates under about $25,000 and set-aside without administration for estates up to $100,000 — so a modest estate may not need a trust. But a single Nevada home can push you past those thresholds quickly, and a trust also manages your affairs if you become incapacitated, which the small-estate shortcuts don't address.
