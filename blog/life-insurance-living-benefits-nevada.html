Life Insurance

The life insurance that pays you while you're still here.

Most people think of life insurance as something their family collects after they're gone. But modern policies can pay a chunk of the benefit to you — if a terminal, chronic, or critical illness hits first. Here's how living benefits actually work for Nevada families.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · October 10, 2026 · 7 min read

When most Nevada families buy life insurance, they picture a single moment: a death, and a check that helps the people left behind. That's still the heart of it. But the life insurance sold today can do something the policies of a generation ago couldn't — it can put money in your hands while you're still alive, if a serious illness arrives before the end does. Those features are called living benefits, and they quietly turn a death-benefit-only contract into something closer to a financial safety net for the hardest years of your life.

If you've already worked through how much coverage you actually need and which type of policy fits, living benefits are the next layer most people never get explained — and the one that changes how useful a policy really is.

What "living benefits" actually means

The technical name is an accelerated death benefit (ADB) rider. In plain English: if you experience a qualifying health event, the insurance company will advance part of your death benefit to you, as cash, before you pass away. You can spend it on anything — medical bills, a wheelchair ramp, in-home help, the mortgage, or simply keeping the lights on during a stretch when you can't work. Whatever you take early is subtracted from what your beneficiaries receive later. It isn't extra money; it's early access to money you already own inside the policy.

What makes this so valuable is when the money shows up. A serious diagnosis rarely waits for a convenient time, and the costs it brings — and the income it takes away — often land years before anyone collects a death benefit. Living benefits are designed to close exactly that gap.

The three kinds of living benefits

Riders vary by carrier, but nearly all living benefits fall into three buckets, each triggered by a different kind of health event:

RiderWhat triggers itTypical use
Terminal illnessA physician certifies a limited life expectancy (often 12–24 months)Treatment, travel, time with family, settling affairs
Chronic illnessYou can't perform 2 of 6 daily activities, or have severe cognitive declineIn-home care, assisted living, caregiving costs
Critical illnessA major event such as heart attack, stroke, or cancer diagnosisCash to cover deductibles, lost income, recovery

A terminal illness benefit is the most common and is built into most modern policies automatically. A chronic illness benefit is the one that overlaps with long-term care — it pays when you lose the ability to handle everyday tasks like bathing, dressing, or eating. A critical illness benefit pays a lump sum on a specific diagnosis, in the same spirit as the standalone critical illness and accident coverage I've written about separately.

Chronic illness rider vs. long-term care rider

This is where people get confused, and where a few minutes of plain explanation saves real money. Both a chronic illness rider and a long-term care rider can help pay for care — but they're built under different sections of the tax code, and they behave differently.

  • A long-term care (LTC) rider is regulated as long-term care insurance (IRC §7702B). It generally offers more robust, predictable funding for qualified care, often on a defined monthly schedule, and must meet consumer-protection standards for LTC products.
  • A chronic illness rider is an accelerated death benefit (IRC §101(g)). It usually pays flexible cash you can spend however you like and can be easier to qualify for, but some versions apply a discount to the accelerated amount or leave the exact payout less certain until you claim.

Neither is "better" in the abstract. If guaranteed, structured care funding is your priority, an LTC rider — or a dedicated policy — usually wins. If flexible cash and simplicity matter more, a chronic illness rider can be the cleaner fit. This is the same crossroads I walk clients through in traditional vs. hybrid long-term care coverage, because the right answer depends on your health, your assets, and what you're really trying to protect.

How the payout — and the taxes — work

When you file a living-benefit claim, the insurer reviews the medical certification and, if it qualifies, releases a portion of the death benefit. How much you can accelerate, and whether the payout is reduced by a discount or a lien, depends entirely on the rider's wording. The key thing to remember: every dollar you take early shrinks the dollars left for your beneficiaries, plus any interest or charges the contract specifies.

On taxes, the news is mostly good. Accelerated benefits paid for a terminal or qualifying chronic illness are generally free of federal income tax under IRC §101(g), and because Nevada has no state income tax, there's no state bite either. Chronic illness benefits are typically tax-free only up to federal per-diem limits, and accepting them can affect eligibility for need-based programs like Medicaid — so this is one place to confirm the details with a tax professional before you rely on a number.

Why this matters in Northern Nevada

Living benefits aren't a Nevada-specific product, but they fill a gap that's especially real here. Nevada has no state disability insurance program — a point I make in detail in what happens to your paycheck if you can't work — so when illness stops a paycheck, there's no state backstop to lean on. For a self-employed contractor in Carson City, a small-business owner in Reno, or a couple stretching a fixed retirement income in the Carson Valley, early access to a life insurance benefit can be the difference between weathering a health crisis and draining the savings they spent decades building.

It also means a single, well-chosen policy can quietly do double duty: protect your family if you die, and protect your own finances if you get seriously sick first. For a lot of Nevada households, that's a far more efficient use of premium than buying three separate products to cover three separate fears.

Two things to check before you count on it

Living benefits are genuinely useful, but the fine print decides how useful. Before you assume a policy has you covered, confirm two things:

  • Which riders you actually have, and what triggers them. A terminal illness benefit may already be in your current policy even if nobody mentioned it. Chronic and critical illness riders usually must be added when the policy is issued — so if you want them, it's often a decision made at purchase (or at conversion), not later.
  • How the payout is calculated. Ask whether the accelerated amount is dollar-for-dollar or discounted, how much of the death benefit you can access, and exactly what medical certification the carrier requires. Two policies with identical premiums can treat a claim very differently.

That review — reading the rider language across carriers and matching it to your health and your goals — is exactly what I do with families across Carson City, Reno, and Northern Nevada. Good questions

Living benefits, answered straight.

What are living benefits on a life insurance policy?

Living benefits are riders that let you access part of your life insurance death benefit while you're still alive if you experience a qualifying health event — most commonly a terminal illness, a chronic illness (losing the ability to perform daily activities), or a serious critical illness such as a heart attack, stroke, or cancer diagnosis. The insurer advances a portion of the death benefit as cash you can use for anything, and whatever you accelerate reduces the amount your beneficiaries receive later. These riders are formally known as accelerated death benefit (ADB) riders.

Are living benefit riders free, or do they cost extra?

It depends on the rider. A terminal illness accelerated death benefit is built into most modern policies at no additional premium. Chronic and critical illness riders may be included at no extra cost on some policies, offered for an added premium on others, or applied as a "discounted" payout where the insurer reduces the accelerated amount in exchange for providing the benefit. Because carriers structure these very differently, comparing the actual rider language across insurers matters more than comparing the headline premium.

How is a living benefit payout taxed in Nevada?

Accelerated death benefits paid for a terminal or qualifying chronic illness are generally received free of federal income tax under Internal Revenue Code section 101(g), and because Nevada has no state income tax there is no state tax either. Chronic illness benefits are typically excluded only up to federal per-diem limits, and large or unusual payouts can have tax consequences, so confirm your specific situation with a tax professional. Accelerating benefits can also affect eligibility for need-based programs like Medicaid.

What's the difference between a chronic illness rider and a long-term care rider?

Both can help pay for care, but they're built under different parts of the law. A long-term care rider (IRC section 7702B) is regulated as long-term care insurance, usually pays for qualified care on a defined schedule, and tends to offer more robust, predictable care funding. A chronic illness rider (IRC section 101(g)) is an accelerated death benefit that pays cash you can spend however you like, often with fewer hurdles but sometimes a discounted payout and less certainty about the amount. Which fits depends on whether your priority is guaranteed care funding or flexible cash.

Can I add living benefits to a life insurance policy I already have?

Sometimes. A terminal illness benefit is often already in your policy even if you've never noticed it — it's worth checking. Chronic and critical illness riders usually have to be added when the policy is issued and can't always be bolted onto an existing contract, though a term policy with a conversion option may let you move into permanent coverage that includes them. The practical step is to have an independent agent review your current policy's riders before you assume you do or don't have them.
