Life Insurance

The exit built into your term policy that almost nobody uses.

Most term life policies let you switch to permanent coverage with no new medical exam — even if your health has changed. Here's how term conversion works, the deadline most Nevadans miss, and when it's genuinely worth it.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · October 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Most people buy term life insurance for a simple reason: it's the cheapest way to cover a big, temporary risk — the mortgage, the years before the kids are grown, the stretch of a career where losing a paycheck would be a disaster. You pick a 20- or 30-year term, lock in a low premium, and get on with your life. What almost nobody reads is the one clause that can matter more than the price: the conversion option.

A conversion option lets you trade your term policy for a permanent one from the same insurer — without a new medical exam and without proving you're still insurable. For a healthy person it's a footnote. For someone whose health has changed since they first applied, it can be the difference between keeping life insurance and losing it entirely. And it runs on a deadline most policyholders never know they have.

1. What the conversion option actually does

Term insurance only pays if you die during the term. When the level-premium period ends, you can usually keep the policy, but the premium starts climbing every year — fast — until it's unaffordable, and then the coverage lapses. For most people that's fine: the mortgage is paid, the kids are launched, and the need is gone. But not always.

The conversion privilege is the escape hatch. It lets you convert some or all of your term coverage into a permanent policy — whole life, universal life, or indexed universal life, depending on what the carrier offers — that lasts your whole life instead of expiring. Crucially, the insurer can't ask you a single new health question. You convert at your current age, but you keep the health classification you were originally approved at. If you were a preferred, non-smoker at 42, that's the class your conversion is priced on, even if you've since been diagnosed with something that would make a brand-new policy expensive or impossible to get.

2. Why this is worth understanding before you need it

Here's the scenario I see most often. Someone bought a 20-year term in their early forties, raised a family, and is now closing in on the end of the term in their early sixties. Along the way they picked up a health condition — nothing dramatic, just the kind of thing that happens to most of us with time. They assume they'll simply buy a new policy when this one ends. Then they apply, and the new underwriting either prices them out or declines them.

The conversion option sidesteps that entirely, because it's guaranteed regardless of health. That guarantee is exactly why it exists — and exactly why it's easy to overlook when you're healthy and the term still has years left. The time to understand it is before a diagnosis, not after, because once your window closes it's gone for good.

3. The deadline most people miss

Conversion isn't open forever. Every policy sets its own window, and the details vary by carrier, so the only truly reliable answer is in your contract. That said, the common patterns look like this:

  • A hard age cutoff. Many policies let you convert only up to a certain age — frequently somewhere between 65 and 70.
  • The end of the level-term period. Some allow conversion only during the years your premium is still level, so a 20-year term may stop being convertible around year 20.
  • A limited early window. A few policies restrict conversion to the first several policy years — the shortest and easiest window to miss.
  • Which products you can convert into. Carriers can limit the permanent policies available for conversion, and that menu can change over time.

Whichever applies to you, the practical takeaway is the same: don't assume. A five-minute look at your policy's conversion provision — or a quick call to confirm it — tells you how much runway you actually have.

If you're healthy and your need is short, shopping a fresh term policy may well beat converting. Figuring out which bucket you're in is most of deciding how much life insurance you actually need at this stage of life.

5. What you're converting into — and why it can do more

Converting isn't just "the same coverage, forever." A permanent policy adds features term never had. It builds cash value that grows tax-deferred and can be borrowed against later. Many permanent policies can carry living-benefit riders that let you tap the death benefit early if a serious illness hits, and some offer a long-term care rider — the same idea behind the hybrid life/LTC policies I've written about. If you're weighing permanent coverage for the first time, the trade-offs are the same ones in term vs. whole life vs. IUL — conversion just lets you reach them without re-qualifying.

It also reframes a question a lot of Nevadans reach in their sixties: do you still need life insurance in retirement? For some the honest answer is no. For others — a surviving spouse's income, an estate to equalize, a business to protect — the answer is yes, and a conversion is often the cleanest way to get there.

6. Why Nevada tilts the math slightly in your favor

Nevada has no state income tax, and that quietly helps permanent life insurance. The cash value inside a permanent policy already grows tax-deferred at the federal level — and here there's no state income tax layered on top of it, or on the retirement income you're coordinating it with. The death benefit itself generally passes to your beneficiaries income-tax-free in every state, but Nevada's lack of a state income or estate tax keeps the whole picture simpler. None of that is a reason to convert on its own, but for a retiree relocating from a higher-tax state to Carson City, Reno, or the Carson Valley, it's one more small point in the plus column.

Good questions

Term conversion questions, answered straight.

What is a term life conversion option?

A conversion option is a feature built into most term life insurance policies that lets you exchange some or all of your term coverage for a permanent policy from the same insurer without taking a new medical exam or proving you're still insurable. Your new permanent premium is based on your age at the time you convert, but it uses the health classification you were originally approved at — so a diagnosis you've received since buying the policy doesn't raise the price or get you declined.

When does my term conversion window close?

It depends on your specific policy. Many term policies allow conversion only until a set age — commonly somewhere between 65 and 70 — or until the end of the level-premium period, whichever comes first, and some limit it to the first several policy years. Because the deadline varies by carrier and contract, the only reliable answer is to read your policy's conversion provision or ask your agent to confirm your exact window before it passes.

Will converting my term policy require a new medical exam?

No. The entire point of the conversion privilege is that it's guaranteed regardless of your current health — no new exam, no new application questions, no new underwriting. That's what makes it so valuable if your health has changed since you first bought coverage. You simply convert at your current age into an available permanent product, keeping your original health class.

Is converting term life insurance worth it?

It's worth it when you have a reason to keep coverage past the term and either can't easily qualify for a new policy or want the features permanent insurance offers — lifelong protection, cash value that grows tax-deferred, or riders that help pay for long-term care. If you're healthy and only need coverage for a few more years, shopping a new term policy may be cheaper. The right move depends on your health, your budget, and why you still need the coverage.
