Income Protection

What happens to your paycheck if you can't work?

Nevada doesn't run a state disability program. Here's what actually replaces your income after an illness or injury, what it costs, and who needs it most.

By Daniel J. Faiella · Licensed Insurance Advisor, Carson City NV · August 10, 2026 · 7 min read

A client in Dayton called me last spring after a ladder fall left him unable to work his trade for four months. He assumed, like most people do, that some kind of state benefit would kick in to cover the gap. It didn't — because Nevada is one of the 45 states with no state disability insurance program at all. No paycheck, no state check, and a mortgage that didn't care either way. Nationally, only about 43% of working Americans carry any private disability insurance, which means the other 57% are one bad diagnosis or accident away from finding out the hard way what their household's real safety net looks like.

Nevada's safety net is thinner than most people assume

Unlike California, New York, New Jersey, Rhode Island, and Hawaii, Nevada has no state disability insurance (SDI) program deducted from paychecks. That leaves three things people mistakenly count on:

  • Workers' compensation only pays if the injury or illness happened on the job. A car accident on the weekend, a slow-developing back condition, or cancer doesn't qualify — even though it stops your paycheck just the same.
  • Social Security Disability (SSDI) exists, but it's built for severe, long-term disability, not a bridge for a few months. Your condition generally has to be expected to last at least a year, and initial claims commonly take many months — sometimes over a year — with a large share of first applications denied outright.
  • Unemployment insurance doesn't apply at all — it's for people who can work and don't have a job, not people who can't work because of their health.

For employees, a group short-term disability benefit at work can fill part of this gap — but plenty of Nevada employers, especially small businesses, don't offer one. For the self-employed and 1099 workforce so common in Reno, Tahoe, and the Carson Valley's trades, hospitality, and gig-economy jobs, there's usually nothing behind the paycheck at all unless they've built it themselves.

The three products people actually use to fill this gap

These aren't competing products — most complete income-protection plans combine two of them:

Pricing is individual — it depends on your age, health, income, and occupation class (a Reno accountant and a Carson Valley roofer are not priced the same way). As a rough starting point, individual disability coverage commonly runs somewhere in the range of 1–3% of your annual income per year, which is a small premium next to the years of paycheck it's built to replace.

Who needs this most in Northern Nevada

Anyone whose household depends on their paycheck, but a few groups feel this gap first: the self-employed and small-business owners across our service area, tradespeople in physically demanding work, dual-income households where either income stopping would be a real problem, and anyone between jobs or nearing Medicare age who's relying on marketplace health coverage without an employer benefits package behind it. If that's you, this is usually one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost pieces of protection available — precisely because so few people ever get around to buying it.

How to actually get covered

1. Start with what your employer already offers, if anything — many people are surprised to learn they have a small group short-term benefit they've never looked at closely. 2. Estimate your household's real monthly minimum — mortgage or rent, insurance, groceries — so you know what you're actually protecting. 3. Decide whether you need short-term coverage, long-term coverage, or both, based on how many months of expenses your savings could realistically cover on their own. 4. Apply while you're healthy — like life and long-term care insurance, disability coverage is medically underwritten, and pricing and eligibility only get harder to secure after a diagnosis.

Good questions

Income protection questions, answered straight.

Does Nevada have state disability insurance?

No. Unlike California, New York, New Jersey, Rhode Island, and Hawaii, Nevada does not run a state disability insurance program. If illness or injury off the job keeps a Nevada worker from earning a paycheck, there is no state benefit to fall back on — only whatever private coverage, employer benefits, or savings they've arranged themselves.

Will Social Security Disability cover me quickly if I can't work?

Not quickly, and not for everyone. SSDI has strict medical criteria — your condition generally must be expected to last at least a year or be terminal — and initial claims commonly take several months to over a year to be approved, with many first applications denied. It is not designed as a short-term paycheck replacement.

What's the difference between short-term and long-term disability insurance?

Short-term disability typically pays a partial income replacement for a matter of weeks up to about a year, often after a short waiting period of a few days to two weeks. Long-term disability picks up after that, with waiting periods commonly around 90 days, and can pay benefits for years or until retirement age depending on the policy. Many people carry both, or a long-term policy alone if their savings can bridge a short absence.

Do I need disability insurance if I'm self-employed?

Self-employed Nevadans usually need it more than employees do, not less — there's no employer-paid sick leave or group disability plan behind you, and workers' comp typically doesn't apply unless the injury happened on a covered job site. If your household depends on your paycheck, an individual disability policy is often the single highest-leverage coverage you can buy.
